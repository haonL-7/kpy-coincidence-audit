A B S T R A C T
Lysine crotonylation (Kcr) and acetylation (Kac) are evolutionarily conserved post-translational modifications 
that alter protein's charge, polarity, and structure. To explore the potential roles and critical modified proteins in 
male reproduction, we employed the affinity enrichment of pan-anti-Kcr and pan-anti-Kac antibodies with high- 
resolution LC-MS/MS from testes tissues of six boars (three at 5 months and three at 24 months of age), 
respectively. As a result, a large number of Kcr and Kac proteins were identified in testicular tissue between two 
groups. Differential analysis further revealed that Kcr proteins were significantly enriched to Gene Ontology 
biological process terms concerning reproductive system development, response to steroid hormone and response 
to hydroxy isoflavone, whereas Kac proteins primarily regulated mitochondria-localized proteins involved in 
testicular metabolism. RPS5, RPS6, CYP17A1, CYP11A1, and CYP19A1, which may act as important regulators of 
testicular development through crotonylation modification. In addition, we observed indications of crosstalk 
between the two modifications, involving shared enzymes (including HDAC1–3), overlapping substrates, and 
CoA donor metabolism. This study performed a large-scale analysis of Kcr and Kac proteins in porcine testis, 
providing a post-translational modification perspective to testis development.
Significance: The testis is a key organ determining male reproductive capacity, and pigs of different ages exhibit 
distinct reproductive performance. Most previous studies have focused on transcriptomic and proteomic dif­
ferences in testes across developmental stages, while post-translational modifications (PTMs) remain poorly 
explored. Here, using affinity enrichment with pan-anti-Kcr and pan-anti-Kac antibodies combined with high- 
resolution LC-MS/MS, we profiled lysine crotonylation (Kcr) and acetylation (Kac) in pig testes. Bioinformatic 
analyses identified CYP17A1, CYP19A1, CYP11A1, RPS5, and RPS6 as potential regulators of testicular matu­
ration via crotonylation, while acetylation was found to regulate metabolic activity by modifying mitochondrial 
proteins. Furthermore, crosstalk between crotonylation and acetylation at the metabolic level was revealed. 
These findings advance our understanding of PTMs in porcine testis development and provide novel data re­
sources for future studies.
1. Introduction
Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are covalent modifications 
that alter protein folding, localization, activity, and interactions with 
other proteins [1]. By introducing diverse chemical groups, PTMs 
remodel chromatin architecture and regulate key biological processes, 
including cellular metabolism, protein degradation, and DNA replica­
tion [2]. To date, more than 400 distinct PTM types have been identified 
in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic systems, such as acetylation, phos­
phorylation, crotonylation, methylation, glycosylation, and ubiquitina­
tion [3]. PTMs frequently target the ε-amino group of lysine residues 
[4]. Notably, aberrant PTMs are implicated in the pathogenesis of 
neurodegenerative diseases, atopic dermatitis, cancer, and diabetes 
[5–7]. Moreover, PTMs are not restricted to histones but are also widely 
distributed across non-histone proteins, where they play critical roles in 
diverse cellular functions [8].

In eukaryotes such as yeast and humans, lysine crotonylation (Kcr) is 
an evolutionarily conserved acylation modification that is abundantly 
enriched at promoter and enhancer regions of transcriptionally active 
genes [9]. Kcr specifically marks active X- and Y-linked genes in post- 
meiotic male germ cells and regulates spermatogenesis, gene tran­
scription, protein biosynthesis, folding, and degradation in males 
[10–12]. Lysine acetylation (Kac) is widespread in both prokaryotic and 
eukaryotic systems and serves as a hallmark of transcriptionally active 
chromatin. Kac is intimately linked to diverse cellular processes, 
including glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, 
cellular development, and bacterial pathogenicity [13–15]. This modi­
fication is catalyzed by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and removed 
by histone deacetylases (HDACs), regulating the dynamic acetylation of 
lysine residues [16].
The testes are responsible for spermatogenesis and androgen secre­
tion [17], and their development directly determines the reproductive 
efficiency and breeding potential of pigs. Age is a key factor influencing 
male fertility. For Landrace pigs, puberty begins at around 5 months, 
with the production of morphologically mature sperm, and sexual 
maturity is reached by 7 to 9 months of age [18]. To date, numerous 
studies have performed transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of 
testicular tissues from pigs at different ages, identifying several key 
candidate genes and proteins, including PIWIL4, SPATA3, SPATA4, 
Aconitate hydratase (ACO2), Carboxypeptidase O (CPO), and 40S ri­
bosomal protein S5 (RPS5) [19–21]. However, studies on PTMs of 
testicular proteins remain very limited. Here, we analyzed protein cro­
tonylation and acetylation in the testes of 5- and 24-month-old pigs, 
corresponding to two age-associated stages of pubertal development and 
adulthood, with a focus on these two key stages rather than constructing 
a continuous developmental atlas. This study provided a comprehensive 
analysis of Kcr and Kac during testicular development and identified 
potential key modified proteins, providing new insights into the regu­
latory roles of PTMs in spermatogenesis in pigs and other farm animals.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Experimental animals
Six Landrace boars were selected from the zhuozhou experimental 
farm in Hebei province: three at 5 and three at 24 months of age (GW_5 
and GW_24, respectively) for two modification group analyses. One boar 
each at 5 and 24 months of age was randomly selected from these 
groups, together with animals at 0, 2, and 48 months for Western blot 
analysis (designated GW_0, GW_2, and GW_48, respectively). Every 
animal lived in the same environment and was in exceptional health. 
Their testicular tissues were taken after lethal electroshock without 
anaesthesia and stored at −80 ◦C for subsequent experiments.
2.2. Protein extraction and trypsin digestion
Each set of samples was lysed by adding a 4-fold volume of lysis 
buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor, 3 μM TSA, 50 mM NAM) and 
sonicated. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 12000g for 10 
min at 4 ◦C, and the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge 
tube for protein concentration determination using a BCA kit 
(Beyotime).
An equal amount of each protein sample was used for enzymatic 
digestion; the volume was adjusted to that of the lysate. The proteins 
were precipitated with 20% trichloroacetic acid for 2 h at 4 ◦C and 
centrifuged at 4500g for 5 min. The precipitate was collected and 
washed 2–3 times with pre-cooled acetone. The protein sample was then 
redissolved in 200 mM TEAB and ultrasonically dispersed. Trypsin was 
added at 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for the first digestion over­
night at 37 ◦C. The sample was reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol for 60 
min at 37 ◦C and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide for 45 min at 
room temperature in darkness. Trypsin was added at 1:100 trypsin-to- 
protein mass ratio for a second 4 h-digestion. Finally, the peptides 
were desalted by Strata X SPE column.
2.3. Enrichment of modified peptides
The tryptic peptides were dissolved in IP buffer solution (100 mM 
NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0), and the su­
pernatant was transferred to a pre-washed crotonylation/acetylation 
resin (PTM-503/104; PTM Bio) and placed at 4 ◦C with gentle shaking 
for overnight incubation. The resin was washed four times with the IP 
buffer solution and twice with deionized water after incubation. The 
bound peptides were eluted thrice with 0.1% TFA, and the eluates were 
collected and freeze-dried under a vacuum. After extraction and 
desalting, according to the C18 ZipTips instructions, the solution was 
freeze-dried under a vacuum for liquid-mass spectrometry.
2.4. LC-MS/MS
The peptides were dissolved in mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid and 
2% acetonitrile) and separated using a NanoElute ultra-high- 
performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system. Mobile phase B 
consisted of 0.1% formic acid in 100% acetonitrile. The gradient was set 
as follows: 6–22% B for 0–40 min, 22–30% B for 40–52 min, 30–80% B 
for 52–56 min, and held at 80% B for 56–60 min, at a flow rate of 450 
nL/min. After UHPLC separation, peptides were ionized using a capil­
lary ion source and analyzed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer 
(Bruker). The ion source voltage was set to 1.75 kV. MS1 spectra were 
acquired over an m/z range of 100–1700 using high-resolution TOF 
analysis. MS/MS spectra were acquired in PASEF mode with up to 10 
PASEF scans per MS1 cycle. Precursor ions with charge states of 2–5 
were selected for fragmentation, and singly charged ions were excluded. 
The dynamic exclusion time was set to 30 s.
2.5. Database search
Secondary mass spectrometry data were retrieved using MaxQuant 
(v1.6.15.0). The following search parameters were used: the Blast_­
Sus_scrofa_9823_PR_20210806.fasta database (49,792 sequences) was 
employed, and a decoy database was generated to estimate the false 
discovery rate (FDR) arising from random matches. The digestion 
method was set as trypsin/P, the number of missed cut sites was set to 
two, the minimum length of the peptide was set to seven amino acid 
residues, the maximum number of peptide modifications was set to five, 
the mass error tolerance of primary parent ions was set to 20 ppm and 
20 ppm for primary fragment ions in the first and main searches, 
respectively, and that of the secondary fragment ions was set to 20 ppm. 
Carbamidomethylation of cysteine (C) was set as a fixed modification. 
Variable modifications included methionine oxidation and protein N- 
terminal acetylation. Separate database searches were performed for 
lysine crotonylation (Kcr) and lysine acetylation (Kac), in which each 
modification was specified as an additional variable modification. The 
FDR for protein identification and peptide-spectrum match (PSM) 
identification was controlled at 1%.
2.6. Quantitative analysis
LFQ intensity values obtained from the database search, representing 
normalized protein abundance across samples, were used for quantifi­
cation. The LFQ intensity (I) for each protein and modification site was 
centered to generate a relative quantitative value (R), calculated as Rij =
Iij / Mean (Ij), where i denotes the sample and j denotes the protein. To 
further eliminate the influence of protein expression levels on modifi­
cation abundance, the relative quantitative value of each modification 
site was divided by the relative quantitative value of its corresponding 
protein to obtain the final normalized modification site quantitation, 
calculated as: R = R_site / R_protein. The reproducibility of the three

2.7. Annotation and enrichment analyses of differentially modified 
proteins
The fold change for each protein or modification site was calculated 
as the ratio of the mean relative abundance values between groups, 
defined as FC = Mean (Rij, i ϵ GW_24) / Mean (Rij, i ϵ GW_5), where R 
denotes the relative quantitative value, i represents the samples, and j 
indicates the protein or modification site. To assess significance, the 
relative quantification values of modification sites were log2- 
transformed for normality and subjected to a t-test, with P < 0.05 
considered statistically significant. Under the premise of P value <0.05, 
a fold change value greater than 1.5 indicated significant up-regulation 
of the modification level, and value less than 1/1.5 indicated significant 
down-regulation of the modification level. We used Eggnog-mapper 
(v2.0) for COG/KOG annotation of differentially modified proteins. 
GO annotation was performed using the UniProt-GOA database. The 
KEGG database was used for a pathway enrichment analysis of these 
proteins. WolF Psort software was used to determine the subcellular 
localization of differentially modified proteins, and subcellular struc­
tures were predicted using WolF Psort. For GO and KEGG enrichment 
analyses, Fisher's exact tests were used to determine the enrichment of 
differentially expressed modified proteins relative to the identified 
proteins, and Fisher's exact test P value <0.05 was considered 
significant.
2.8. Protein-protein interaction network analysis
The differentially modified protein database numbers or protein se­
quences obtained from filtering were compared with the STRING 
(v.11.0) protein interaction network database, and a confidence score of 
>0.7 was set to obtain differentially modified protein interactions. Next, 
the degree was calculated and the interaction network was visualized 
using Cytoscape (v. 3.10). Hub proteins were further identified using the 
CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape based on degree centrality, and the top 
10 hub proteins were selected.
2.9. Motif analysis
Using peptide sequences comprising 10 amino acids upstream and 10 
amino acids downstream of all potential modification sites in the spe­
cies, motif features were analyzed using Motif-x. The characteristic 
sequence form was considered a type of motif for the modified peptide 
when it featured more than 20 peptides and the P-value was less than 
1.0 × 10−6. The amino acid sequences on either side of the modification 
site were visualized using heatmaps created using the heatmap R 
package.
2.10. Western blot analysis
Western blotting was performed following previously reported 
methods [11]. From testicular tissue, 20 μg of protein was extracted, 
separated using 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel elec­
trophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The 
membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk powder (1× TBST) for 1 h at 
room temperature. An anti-crotonyl-lysine/acetyl-lysine antibody 
(1:1000; PTM BioLabs, PTM-502/101) served as the primary antibody, 
followed by peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) sec­
ondary antibody (1:10000; Thermo, 31430). After incubation, a 
chemiluminescent HRP substrate was added, and the signal was 
captured using a fully automated chemiluminescent image analysis 
system (Tanon, 5200).
3. Results
3.1. The prevalence of Kcr and Kac modifications in porcine testes
We performed Western blotting to assess the presence of Kcr and Kac 
modifications in pig testicular tissues across different developmental 
stages, including newborn piglets (0 month), 2, 5, 24, and 48 months 
old. Multiple bands were observed in each lane (Fig. 1A–1B), suggesting 
that a wide range of testicular proteins underwent Kcr and Kac modifi­
cations throughout development. Subsequently, a combination of highly 
sensitive immunoaffinity antibodies and high-resolution LC-MS/MS was 
used to characterize Kcr and Kac protein modifications in testes at the 
GW_5 and GW_24 stages. The mass counting errors of all identified Kcr 
and Kac modified peptides were less than 20 ppm (Fig. 1C), and most 
peptides were 7–20 amino acids (Fig. 1D), supporting the reliability of 
the mass spectrometry results. Principal component analysis showed a 
clear separation between GW_5 and GW_24 and clustering within each 
group (Supplemental Fig. 1A–B). The average Pearson correlation co­
efficients between the two groups of Kcr samples were 0.972 for the 
GW_5 group and 0.943 for the GW_24 group, while the samples for Kac 
modification were 0.969 and 0.967 for the GW_5 and GW_24 groups 
(Fig. 1E), indicating that the three biological replicates of the two 
modifications showed high reproducibility. Through dataset search and 
filtering, we identified 4519 Kcr proteins, 22477 Kcr sites, 1757 Kac 
proteins, and 4234 Kac sites in six testicular tissues, including histones 
and non-histones (Table S1). An average of 5.0 Kcr modifications 
occurred per Kcr protein and 2.4 Kac modifications per Kac protein 
identified. In addition, 1987 sites were shared between Kcr and Kac 
(Fig. 1F), suggesting potential competitive modifications at the same 
amino acid residues.
3.2. Functional annotation and enrichment analysis of differentially Kcr 
proteins
We identified 222 up-regulated Kcr proteins (274 sites) and 129 
down-regulated Kcr proteins (161 sites) (Fig. 2A), based on the defined 
thresholds. Among these differentially Kcr proteins, approximately 
80.61%, 13.03%, and 3.64% contained one, two, or three Kcr sites, 
respectively (Supplemental Fig. 1C). The majority of differentially Kcr 
proteins were related to crucial transport and metabolic processes, such 
as energy production and conversion, lipid, carbohydrate, and amino 
acid metabolism (Fig. 2B). In terms of subcellular location, most of the 
differential Kcr proteins were predicted to be located in the cytoplasm 
(40.91%), nucleus (22.73%), and mitochondria (14.55%) (Fig. 2C). GO 
enrichment analysis revealed that the up-regulated Kcr proteins were 
predominantly enriched in the reproductive development process and 
response to steroid hormone, involving proteins such as CYP11A1, 
CYP17A1, and HSD17B (Fig. 2D). Down-regulated proteins were pri­
marily enriched the response to hydroxy isoflavone, positive regulation 
of intracellular transport, and response to unfolded protein (Fig. 2D). 
The KEGG pathways associated with these proteins include the ribo­
some, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, protein processing in the 
endoplasmic reticulum, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, 
most Kcr proteins with high fold changes were associated with male 
reproduction (Table 1), for instance, PSMA8 is specifically expressed in 
spermatogonia and regulates the meiotic recombination process during 
spermatogenesis.

3.4. PPI network analysis based on differential modified proteins
To further identify core proteins among the extensive differential 
proteins and pathways, we constructed the PPI network for the two 
modifications. For Kcr, we selected protein interactions belonging to the 
Kcr dataset and excluded external candidates to plot the Kcr differential 
protein PPI map (Fig. 4A). The protein cluster with the tightest in­
teractions was identified as ribosome-associated proteins, consisting of 
22 ribosome-associated crotonylated proteins. Among them, several 
proteins including RPS27A and RPS5 were identified as hub proteins 
that exhibited significant crotonylation and interacted closely with 
other ribosomal family members (Table S1). In addition, heat shock 
proteins (HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, HSPE1, etc.), proteasome family 
members (PSMA8, PSMC6, and PSMD7, etc.) and CYP family members 
(CYP17A1, CYP19A1, and CYP11A1) collectively form a complex PPI 
network (Supplemental Fig. 1F). Similarly, the analysis of two inde­
pendent PPI networks based on Kac differentially proteins showed that 
the core protein clusters were mainly various oxidoreductases localized 
in mitochondria, which are involved in metabolic processes such as the 
TCA cycle and fatty acid β-oxidation to produce energy (Fig. 4B). Among 
them, acetylated proteins such as MDH2, CS, ACO2, and HADH 
exhibited high connectivity and interacted with multiple metabolism- 
related proteins, indicating their potential roles as hub proteins within 
the testicular metabolic network.
3.5. Distribution features of kcr and kac motifs
To investigate the sequence features around Kcr and Kac sites, we 
evaluated the sequence motifs of all of the identified Kcr and Kac pep­
tides using motif-x. The 16 conserved Kcr motifs obtained from 22,068 
surrounding sequences, including KLKcr, V…Kcr.....K…V, Kcr..E..R, 
Kcr..E…K, E.AKcr, K.....L…Kcr, and HKcr....E, and 10 conserved motifs 
filtered from the Kac sequences, such as GKacT, AKacT, GKacP, GKacH, 
GKacG, and GKacS (Fig. 4C, D), which were of varying abundance 
(Table S2). As shown in a heatmap (Fig. 4E), particular amino acid 
residues, such as alanine (A), aspartic acid (D), glutamine (E), lysine (K), 
arginine (R), and valine (V), were more abundant in the region around 
Kcr sites, and most of these amino acid residues were aliphatic. Within 
Kcr sites, the hydrophobic reside alanine (A) was significantly enriched 
from position −10 to +10. Alkaline residues K and R also showed sig­
nificant enrichment from −10 to −5 and + 5 to +10, and acidic residues 
(D or E) were frequently observed in positions other than −1. A variety 
of amino acid residues, including glycine (G), histidine (H), serine (S), 
threonine (T), were primarily found in the −1 and + 1 positions around 
the Kac site. Additionally, hydrophobic groups, such as A, V, etc., were 
enriched in the −10 to +10 positions surrounding Kac.
3.6. Crosstalk between Kcr and Kac proteins
To understand the crosstalk between the two modifications, we 
examined it from the following three aspects. Firstly, Kcr and Kac shared 
the same enzymatic system [32]. At the GW_5 and GW_24 stages, we 
detected the expression of deacetylases (HDAC1, HDAC2, and HDAC3) 
and the acetyltransferase p300 (Table S1), which are capable of medi­
ating both crotonylation and acetylation. Secondly, the two modifica­
tions competed for the same substrates, with 1077 proteins carrying 
both Kcr and Kac marks (Fig. 1F). The majority of these proteins are non- 
histones. Within histones, only histone H4 underwent concurrent cro­
tonylation and acetylation modifications, encompassing five Kcr sites 
(K32, K60, K78, K80, and K92) and two Kac sites (K6 and K17) (Fig. 5A). 
Histone H3 had a single Kcr site (K37), while histone H2B exhibited six 
down-regulated Kac sites (K6, K12, K16, K17, K70, and K132), with no 
differential Kcr sites detected. Thirdly, the proteins with overlapping 
modification were enriched in pathways such as pyruvate metabolism, 
NADP metabolism, and fatty acid β-oxidation, indicating that the 
competition between acetyl and crotonyl groups mostly occurred on 
mitochondrial proteins associated with metabolic pathways (Supple­
mental Fig. 1G). These metabolic processes simultaneously provide key 
sources of crotonyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA, suggesting that the crosstalk 
between crotonylation and acetylation is closely associated with meta­
bolism (Fig. 5B).
4. Discussion
The testis is an essential organ of the male reproductive system, and 
boars of different ages show distinct reproductive capacities. Kcr and 
Kac are two key PTMs, with Kcr involved in spermatogenesis across 
mammals and crustaceans and in the regulation of mouse embryonic 
development, while Kac is essential for chromatin remodeling during 
spermatogenesis [10,33,34]. However, studies on these modifications in 
pig testicular development are limited, and we conducted this research 
to explore their roles. In our identification, a large number of Kcr pro­
teins were present in testicular tissues, with 164 Kcr sites and 139 Kcr 
proteins differing by more than 1.5-fold across developmental stages. 
Similar to Kcr, we identified a large number of Kac proteins in the testes, 
and the temporal specificity of these protein modification levels is re­
flected in testicular development.
Several GO terms related to male reproduction were identified, such 
as steroid hormone biosynthesis, reproductive structure and system 
development, gametogenesis. Additionally, the potential role of croto­
nylation and acetylation in gonadal hormone synthesis has also been 
captured. Among the Kcr proteins involved in these pathways, CYP17A1 
and CYP11A1 are highly expressed in Leydig cells and function as key 
enzymes in gonadotropin synthesis, both catalyzing the production of 
endogenous androgens that stimulate the development of the seminif­
erous tubules and spermatogenesis [35,36]. CYP19A1, an aromatase 
that catalyzes A-ring aromatization, converts testosterone to estradiol, 
producing low levels of estrogen in males to maintain homeostasis [37]. 
Ribosomal proteins are among the most highly expressed genes in 
almost all cells, and their products influence protein folding [38]. 
Among them, RPS5 is a key RNA-binding component, and its low 
expression has been associated with male oligozoospermia [39]. Our 
findings highlight RPS5 as a hub protein in ribosomal PPI, consistent

There was a significant deviation in the distribution of amino acids at 
the −10 to +10 positions around the Kcr sites, with a preference for 
aliphatic moieties (A) and acidic amino acids (D, E). The active presence 
of D and E near Kcr sites appears to be a general characteristic of Kcr 
modifications [4,41]. In contrast, amino acid residues such as A, G, and 
H mostly appear from −1 to +1 in the Kac sites with no preference for 
acidic or basic groups. Besides the ribosomal protein cluster, the PPI 
network of differential Kcr proteins also includes members of the HSP 
and CYP families. Ribosomal protein PPIs have also been reported in Kcr 
analyses of many plant taxa [1,42] and microorganisms [43], directing 
the biosynthesis of polypeptide chains. Differential Kac proteins form 
PPIs with oxidoreductases involved in the tricarboxylic acid cycle and 
other metabolic processes that contribute to energy production during 
spermatogenesis.
In our results, 61.3% of Kac proteins overlapped with Kcr proteins, 
leading to similar annotations. However, distinct modification sites and 
proteins resulted in divergent functional mappings, which fulfill the 
requirements of complex biological functions. Previous studies have 
demonstrated that histone acetylation and crotonylation share the same 
enzyme system, for example, KAT family members such as lysine 
acetyltransferases p300/CBP, PCAF, and hMOF, have crotonyltransfer­
ase activities, and catalytically crotonylated proteins can directly stim­
ulate the transcription process [44]. Other common KDACs, such as the 
lysine deacetylases HDAC1–3 and Sirtuin family members SIRT1–3, are 
equally capable of removing crotonylation in a site-specific manner, and 
the whole process is not limited by whether the substrate is a histone or 
non-histone, whereas the extent to which the same substrate undergoes 
the two types of acylation is mainly dependent on the relative concen­
trations of intracellular crotonyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA [45,46]. PTMs can 
regulate the function of mitochondrial proteins, especially playing roles 
in key metabolic processes such as fatty acid β-oxidation and pyruvate 
metabolism [40]. These metabolic pathways are also major sources of 
crotonyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA, revealing a potential crosstalk between 
Kcr and Kac at the metabolic level.
A major limitation of this study is that it is primarily based on pro­
teomic and bioinformatic analyses, without direct phenotypic evidence 
such as testicular histology or hormone measurements, which poses 
challenges for precisely defining testicular developmental stages. In 
addition, although key crotonylated proteins (e.g., CYP17A1, CYP11A1, 
CYP19A1) exhibited significant modification changes, site-specific 
validation and functional experiments were not performed. Future 
studies will focus on mechanistic validation of these findings.

5. Conclusion
In summary, we delineated the proteomic landscape of crotonylation 
and acetylation in pig testes across male reproductive development. 
Several potential differentially crotonylated factors were identified, and 
the crosstalk between Kcr and Kac was preliminarily explored, providing 
new insights into the biological roles of these two post-translational 
modifications.
