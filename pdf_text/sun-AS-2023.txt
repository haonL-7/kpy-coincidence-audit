A B S T R A C T  
Background and aims: Arterial calcification is the predictor of cardiovascular risk in diabetic patients. Nε-car­
boxymethyl-lysine (CML), a toxic metabolite, is associated with accelerated vascular calcification in diabetes 
mellitus (DM). However, the mechanism remains elusive. This study aims to explore the key regulators involved 
in CML-induced vascular calcification in DM. 
Methods: We used Western blot and immuno-staining to test the expression and localization of nuclear factor of 
activated T cells, cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1) in human samples, a diabetic apolipoprotein E-deficient (ApoE−/−) 
mouse model, and a vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC) model. Further, we confirmed the regulator of NFATc1 
phosphorylation and acetylation induced by CML. The role of NFATc1 in VSMCs calcification and osteogenic 
differentiation was explored in vivo and in vitro. 
Results: In diabetic patients, CML and NFATc1 levels increased in the severe calcified anterior tibial arteries. CML 
significantly promoted NFATc1 expression and nuclear translocation in VSMCs and mouse aorta. Knockdown of 
NFATc1 significantly inhibited CML-induced calcification. CML promoted NFATc1 acetylation at K549 by 
downregulating sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), which antagonized the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) induced NFATc1 phos­
phorylation at the Y270 site. FAK and SIRT3 affected the nuclear translocation of NFATc1 by regulating the 
acetylation-phosphorylation crosstalk. NFATc1 dephosphorylation mutant Y270F and deacetylation mutant 
K549R had opposite effects on VSMC calcification. SIRT3 overexpression and FAK inhibitor could reverse CML- 
promoted VSMC calcification. 
Conclusions: CML enhances vascular calcification in DM through NFATc1. In this process, CML increases NFATc1 
acetylation by downregulating SIRT3 to antagonize FAK-induced NFATc1 phosphorylation.   
1. Introduction 
Vascular calcification is a reliable predictor for the risk of major 
adverse cardiovascular events, confirmed by a large number of clinical 
data [1–3]. Further studies have refined the classification according to 
the morphology and location of calcification [1–4]. It is clear that 
increased calcification is closely related to mortality in patients with 
cardiovascular disease [5]. Due to the long-term high blood glucose 
level, there is a tendency for formation of toxic glycolipid metabolites in 
diabetics, which greatly contribute to vascular calcification develop­
ment and promote the occurrence of acute cardiovascular events [6,7]. 
Therefore, there is an urgent need to gain a better understanding of the

causes and molecular mechanisms of vascular calcification in diabetes

Advanced glycation end products (AGEs), formed in the Maillard 
reaction, are one of the most common diabetic toxic metabolites [8]. 
Nε-carboxymethyl-lysine (CML) is an active component of AGEs. A 
higher level of circulating CMLwas associated with increased cardio­
vascular events [9]. Previous studies also revealed that CML was 
involved in the occurrence and progression of diabetic nephropathy [10] 
and peripheral vascular disease [11]. Our former studies investigated 
the role of CML in promoting vascular calcification in DM. CML could 
directly act on vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) to promote oste­
ogenic and adipogenic differentiation [12], and CML could also stimu­
late the release of apoptotic bodies to mediate VSMC calcification [13]. 
However, the molecular mechanisms involved in CML-induced promo­
tion of vascular calcification in DM are still unclear. 
Nuclear factor of activated T cells, cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1) is a 
member of the NFAT transcription factors family and a key regulator of 
numerous pathophysiological processes [14,15]. Nuclear translocation 
and transcriptional regulation of NFATc1 can mediate changes of mul­
tiple indicators at the mRNA level [16]. Previous studies have shown 
that NFATc1 related cascade signals play a critical role in the progres­
sion of diabetic osteoporosis [17] and diabetic nephropathy [18]. More 
importantly, NFATc1 has been reported to participate in the balance 
mediation of osteogenic-osteoclastic metabolism [19,20]. In the regu­
lation of osteoclast activity, NFATc1 is an essential indicator of osteo­
clastic differentiation [21]. However, whether NFATc1 plays a 
regulatory role in vascular calcification under the toxic metabolic 
environment of DM needs further study. 
In this study, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to screen the 
potential regulatory factor of vascular calcification in DM and further 
verified NFATc1 in the anterior tibial arteries from patients with dia­
betic foot amputated, and in vitro and in vivo models. Then, we explored 
the regulatory mechanism of VSMC osteogenic differentiation and 
vascular calcification under CML stimulation in diabetic apolipoprotein 
E-deficient (ApoE−/−) mice and primary murine VSMCs. We hope to 
provide a breakthrough point for the prevention and treatment of 
vascular calcification in DM. 
2. Patients and methods 
2.1. Patients 
All clinical studies complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and 
were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of 
Jiangsu University. Anterior tibial arteries were collected from 19 pa­
tients with an amputated diabetic foot. All amputations were performed 
in the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University from November 2019 to 
March 2021, and these patients had given prior informed consent. 
Fasting blood was also collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 30 min 
to obtain serum. CML in serum and artery tissues was determined with 
an ELISA kit (CEB977Ge, Cloud-Clone, Wuhan, China), and the experi­
mental steps were performed in accordance with the instructions. After 
the calcium content of artery specimens was determined with a Calcium 
Colorimetric Assay Kit (S1063S, Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China), 
the patients were divided into the mild calcification group (9 patients) 
and severe calcification group (10 patients) according to the median 
calcium content (8.5mol/gprot). Then, the anterior tibial arteries were 
sectioned for subsequent staining. 
2.2. Animals 
Six-week-old male ApoE−/−
mice and C57 BL/6 mice (Cavens 
Experimental Animals, Changzhou, China) were intraperitoneally 
injected with streptozotocin (STZ) (40 mg/kg/day) for 5 consecutive 
days to construct the in vivo diabetic model [3,13]. Two weeks later, 
mice with a blood glucose level of more than 300 mg/dL were used for 
the subsequent experiments. Additional CML was supplemented via tail 
vein injection (10 mg/kg/day) (Toronto research chemicals, Toronto, 
Canada). Adeno-associated virus (AAV) (1010v.g/mouse) (Sangon 
Biotech, Shanghai, China) or recombinant adenovirus (Ad) (4 × 109 pfu) 
(GenePharma, Shanghai, China) was used to silence or overexpress 
NFATc1. The viral vector was used as negative control (AAVshNC was 
the control of AAVshNFATc1 and AdNC was the control of AdNFATc1). 
Mice were then fed a high-fat diet (41 kcal% fat and 43 kcal% carbo­
hydrates) (HFK Bioscience, Beijing, China) for 18 weeks (Supplementary 
Fig. S1). All mice were randomly divided into different groups and 
housed in an environment of 12h light/12h dark cycle and 22 ◦C tem­
perature and had free access to food and water. Mice were euthanized 
with 100 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital injection at the end of the 
experiment. The thoracic aorta was isolated for subsequent experiments. 
The sample size was chosen to ensure reproducibility and allow strin­
gent statistical analysis. All animal experiments complied with the 
ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Experimental Animal Use 
Ethics Committee of Jiangsu University. 
2.3. Cell cultures 
Primary mouse VSMCs were isolated as previously described [2,3]. 
In short, aortas from 6-week-old male C57 BL/6 mice were obtained 
immediately after euthanasia. After removing the adventitia and intima, 
the aortic medium was cut into a 0.1 × 0.1 cm tissue block. Then, the 
tissue block was moved into DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine 
serum (FBS) (Wisent Biotech, Nanjing, China), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 
100 μg/mL streptomycin (Wisent Biotech). After the cells migrated from 
the tissue block, the cell culture medium was updated. Cells after the 3rd 
passage were used for subsequent experiments. Osteogenic medium 
(OM) containing 2.5 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate and 50 μg/mL ascor­
bic acid was added and cultured for 7 days to induce osteogenic dif­
ferentiation of VSMCs (Supplementary Fig. S2A). CML and 50 μg/mL 
oxidized low density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) were used to simulate glucose 
and lipid metabolism disorders in DM. NFATc1 and sirtuin (SIRT) 3 were 
overexpressed or knocked down with plasmid or siRNA transduction 
(Supplementary Figs. S2B and C). The focal adhesion kinase (FAK) ac­
tivity was inhibited with inhibitor Y15 (1 μmol/L) (MedChemExpress, 
New Jersey, USA). 
2.4. Calcification measurement 
For von Kossa staining, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde 
for 30 min. After washing, samples were placed in 2.5% silver nitrate 
solution and then exposed to ultraviolet light for 2 h. Then 5% sodium 
thiosulfate solution was added to neutralize the unreacted silver nitrate. 
After washing, samples were stained with 1% neutral red for 3 min. 
Finally, calcium nodules were observed and photographed under a mi­
croscope (IX51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For Alizarin Red S staining of 
cell samples, after fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, 2% Alizarin Red 
(pH 4.2) (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) was added and 
incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After washing, the sample 
was photographed. For tissues, the whole mouse aorta was obtained and 
fixed in 95% ethanol overnight. The aorta was then incubated with 
0.003% Alizarin Red S dissolved in 1% potassium hydroxide overnight. 
After washing with 2% potassium hydroxide, the sample was photo­
graphed. The o-cresolphthalein complexone method was applied for 
calcium content determination, and a Calcium Colorimetric Assay Kit 
(Beyotime Biotech) was used. The protein concentration of samples was 
detected with the BCA Protein Quantification Kit (E112, Vazyme 
Biotech, Nanjing, China). Calcium content was standardized to protein 
concentration. All experimental steps were carried out according to the 
instructions of the kits.

2.5. Quantitative PCR (qPCR), Western blot and immunoprecipitation 
For qPCR, total RNA was extracted with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, 
CA, USA). The mRNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA with a 
Reverse Transcriptase Kit (R222, Vazyme Biotech). QPCR was per­
formed using the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q311, Vazyme Biotech). The 
experiments were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s 
instructions. All primer sequences used are shown in Table 1. β-actin was 
used as internal reference. For Western blot, tissue/cell protein extracts 
were obtained with the protein lysate (RIPA: protease inhibitor: phos­
phatase inhibitor 100:1:1 in volume) (Beyotime Biotech). After the 
sample was fully lysed, 5 × loading buffer was added and boiled for 8 
min. Proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred to 
a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Merck Millipore, Billerica, 
USA). After the membrane was blocked with 10% skimmed milk and 
incubated with the primary antibody and secondary antibody in 
sequence, a chemiluminescence system (Amersham Imager 600, General 
Electric, Boston, USA) was applied to display and analyze the images. 
The primary antibody used in Western blot was anti-CML (1:2000, 
ab125145, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-β-actin (1:5000, 21800, Sig­
nalway Antibody, Maryland, USA), anti-NFATc1 (1:1000, ab125145, 
Abcam), anti-Histone H3 (H3) (1:1000, ab176842, Abcam), anti- 
Phospho-Serine(Ser)/Threonine (Thr) (1:1000, AP1067, ABclonal, 
Wuhan, China), anti-Phospho-Tyrosine(Tyr, Y) (1:1000, PTM-702RM, 
PTM-Biolab, Hangzhou, China), anti-Acetyllysine (1:1000, PTM-101, 
PTM-Biolab), anti-SIRT3 (1:1000, AF5135, Affinity Biosciences, 
Changzhou, China), anti-FAK (1:3000, 66258-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, 
China), Anti-GFP tag (1:5000, 66002-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Flag Tag 
(1:5000, M1403-2, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-Bcl-2 associated X

(1:1000, 10380-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-actin, alpha 2, smooth mus­
cle, aorta (α-SMA) (1:1000, ER1003, HuaBio).Anti-Runt-related tran­
scription factor 2 (RUNX2) (1:1000, ab236639, Abcam) and anti-Bone 
morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) (1:1000, 18933-1-AP, Proteintech). 
Immunoprecipitation was performed with a Protein A/G Immunopre­
cipitation Kit (22202, Beaverbio, Suzhou, China). All operations were 
performed in accordance with the instructions of the kit. Briefly, cells 
were lysed to prepare protein suspensions. The primary antibody was 
incubated with magnetic beads at room temperature for 30 min. Then, 
the antibody-magnetic bead conjugate was added to the protein sus­
pension and incubated for 30 min. Subsequently, immunoprecipitates 
were obtained using a magnetic frame and analyzed by Western blot. 
The primary antibodies used in immunoprecipitation were anti-NFATc1 
(1:200, sc-7294, Santa Cruz Biotech, Dallas, USA), anti-SIRT3 (1:200, sc- 
365175, Santa Cruz), anti-FAK (1:100, 66258-1-Ig, Proteintech) and 
anti-GFP tag (1:100, 66002-1-Ig, Proteintech). 
2.6. Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining 
For immunohistochemical staining, tissue sections were placed into 
4% paraformaldehyde and fixed at room temperature for 30 min. Then 
the SP Rabbit & Mouse HRP kit (CW2069, CoWin Century Biotech, 
Beijing, China) was applied, and the experimental steps were performed 
in accordance with the kit instructions. Then, sections were stained with 
hematoxylin for 2 min to visualize the nucleus, and they were finally 
observed under the microscope (Olympus). Images were analyzed using 
ImageJ software. The primary antibody used was anti-NFATc1 (1:200, 
sc-7294, Santa Cruz). For immunofluorescence staining, after the sam­
ples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, they were placed in the 
blocking solution supplemented with 5% goat serum and 0.1% Triton X- 
100 and incubated for 30 min. After washing, primary antibodies were 
added and incubated at 4 ◦C overnight. The samples were then incu­
bated with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies for 30 min. The 
nuclei were marked with DAPI (1:10000, D9542, Sigma-Aldrich, St. 
Louis, USA). Finally, samples were observed and photographed with a 
microscope (Olympus) or a confocal microscope (LSM510, Carl Zeiss, 
Thornwood, USA). Images were analyzed with Image-Pro Plus software. 
2.7. Plasmids and small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection and 
luciferase reporter assay 
To overexpress NFATc1 and SIRT3, the pCMV6-AC-GFP-NFATc1 
(General Biol, Chuzhou, China) and pCMV-Flag-SIRT3 (Generay 
Biotech, Shanghai, China) plasmids were constructed. SiRNA targeting 
NFATc1 or SIRT3 (GenePharma) was synthesized to knock down 
NFATc1 and SIRT3 in cells. To verify the phosphorylation and acetyla­
tion sites of NFATc1, pCMV6-AC-GFP-NFATc1 Y270F and pCMV6-AC- 
GFP-NFATc1 K549R mutant plasmids (General Biol) were constructed. 
Plasmid and siRNA transfection were performed when cell density 
reached 70–80% confluence. Opti-MEM® Medium (Gibco, New York, 
USA) and Lipo8000™ reagent (C0533, Beyotime Biotech) were used to 
enhance the transfection efficiency. Experimental steps followed the 
instructions of Lipo8000™ reagent. For luciferase reporter assay, VSMCs 
were seeded in a 96-well plate at the density of 1 × 104 cells and 
transfected with BMP2 promoter reporter plasmids (General Biol) or 
pGL3-basic plasmids. Renilla plasmids were also transfected to 
normalize luciferase activities. After 24 h, cells were lysed to detect 
luminescence with the Duo-Lite Luciferase Assay System (Vazyme 
Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 
2.8. Mass spectrometry (MS) 
Cell extracts from CML-stimulated VSMCs were subjected to immu­
noprecipitation with anti-NFATc1. The immunoprecipitates were sepa­
rated by SDS-PAGE, which was subsequently excised after staining with 
Coomassie brilliant blue. After dehydration, the gel strips were digested 
with 5 ng/μL trypsin at 37 ◦C for 20 h. Smples after enzymatic hydrolysis 
were desalted and centrifuged in vacuum, redissolved in 0.2% formic 
acid and loaded onto a reversed-phase analytical column (Thermo 
Fisher, MA, USA, 75 μm × 150 mm, packed with Acclaim PepMap RSLC 
C18; 2 μm, 100 Å, nanoViper) and subsequently separated by gradient 
elution buffer with the Easy nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher). The 
eluted peptides were detected with the nanospray ionization linear ion 
trap tandem MS (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher). Twenty fragments (MS2 
scan) were collected after each full scan. Mascot software was used for 
data analysis to obtain qualitative identification information of target 
protein polypeptide molecules.

the RNA was qualified, the mRNA was enriched with magnetic beads

2.11. Statistical analysis 
Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to detect the normality of data. Data of 
normal distribution were shown by mean ± SD. The comparison be­
tween two groups was performed by the Student t-test. One way ANOVA 
followed by Turkey’s post hoc test was used to analyze three or more 
groups. Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the correlation 
between groups. Data showing with non-normality were displayed with 
median and interquartile spacing, and the comparison between groups 
was performed with the nonparametric test. SPSS 22.0 software was 
used for statistical analysis. p < 0.05 represented a statistically signifi­
cant difference.

3.2. CML promotes NFATc1 expression and nuclear translocation 
We established a mouse aortic smooth muscle cell model of diabetic 
vascular calcification and a mouse model of diabetic vascular calcifi­
cation [2,3]. In the cell model, protein levels of NFATc1 were increased 
in a CML concentration dependent manner (Fig. 2A and B). We noticed 
that 20 μmol/L CML reversely induced a decrease in the NFATc1 level, 
which may be caused by the cytotoxicity of high concentration of CML. 
Therefore, 10 μmol/L CML was selected as the concentration of the 
subsequent experiments. Previous studies have demonstrated the 
pro-apoptosis effects of CML. In this study, we also found that CML 
promoted the expression of apoptosis indicators Caspase9 and BAX in 
calcified VSMCs (Supplementary Fig. S4). nfatc1 mRNA level was also 
upregulated in the CML group (Fig. 2C). NFATc1 is a member of the 
transcription factor NFAT family. NFATc1 nuclear translocation de­
termines its transcriptional regulatory activity [19]. We used laser 
confocal microscopy to observe the localization of NFATc1 in VSMCs 
(Fig. 2D) and found that CML significantly increased NFATc1 expression 
(Fig. 2E) and nuclear translocation (Fig. 2F). Western blot also showed 
that CML significantly increased the level of NFATc1 in the nuclear ly­
sates (Fig. 2G and H), while the content of NFATc1 in the cytoplasm was 
significantly decreased (Supplementary Fig. S5). We observed similar 
results in the ApoE−/−mouse model. Western blot (Fig. 2I and J) and 
immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 2K and L) showed that the expres­
sion of NFATc1 in the mouse aorta was significantly increased in the DM 
+ CML group. We then fluorescently labeled NFATc1 and α-SMA 
(Fig. 2M). CML greatly promoted NFATc1 expression and nuclear 
translocation in smooth muscle cells (Fig. 2N and O). These results 
suggested that the expression of NFATc1 and the transcriptional regu­
latory activity were enhanced by CML. 
3.3. CML increases osteogenic differentiation and vascular calcification 
via upregulating NFATc1 
In the diabetic models of wild-type (WT) and ApoE−/−
mice, the 
expression of RUNX2 and BMP2, indicators of osteogenic differentiation 
[24] was significantly increased in the DM + CML group (Fig. 3A). AAV 
(shNFATc1) or Ad (AdNFATc1) were used to knockdown or overexpress 
NFATc1 in the aorta of diabetic ApoE−/−mice, respectively. Knockdown 
of NFATc1 with AAV-shNFATc1 significantly reduced the expression of 
RUNX2 and BMP2 (Fig. 3B). Overexpression of NFATc1 further 
enhanced the expression of osteogenic differentiation markers (Fig. 3C). 
Calcium content assay and von Kossa staining indicated that CML could 
promote aortic calcification in diabetic ApoE−/−mice (Fig. 3D and E). 
The increase in plaque calcification induced by CML was reversed after 
inhibition of NFATc1 with AAV-shNFATc1, while the overexpression of 
NFATc1 by AdNFATc1 further promoted plaque calcification (Fig. 3D 
and E). In the cell model, NFATc1 was knocked down or overexpressed 
with siRNA or plasmids, respectively. The knockdown of NFATc1 
significantly downregulated the expression of RUNX2 and BMP2 
(Fig. 3F–H) and upregulated the expression of contraction phenotypic 
index α-SMA in VSMCs (Supplementary Fig. S6A), suggesting that the 
inhibition of NFATc1 could antagonize CML-induced osteogenic differ­
entiation of VSMCs. After overexpression of NFATc1, the osteogenic 
differentiation of VSMCs was further aggravated (Fig. 3I–K, Supple­
mentary Fig. S6B). Alizarin Red S staining and calcium content assay 
showed that CML significantly increased mineralization deposition in 
VSMCs, which was reversed by the knockdown of NFATc1 with siRNA 
(Fig. 3L and M). Compared with the CML + Vector group, the degree of 
calcification was further increased after overexpression of NFATc1 
(CML + NFATc1 group) (Fig. 3L and M). These results suggested that 
CML promoted vascular calcification and osteogenic differentiation of 
VSMCs via upregulating NFATc1.

(Supplementary Fig. S8). MS revealed that SIRT3 may bind to NFATc1.

3.5. FAK is involved in the phosphorylation of NFATc1 
The phosphorylation of proteins is closely associated with their ki­
nases. MS found that FAK, a kinase involved in the phosphorylation of 
Tyr, may be a potential driver of NFATc1 phosphorylation (Fig. 5A). The 
interaction between FAK and NFATc1 was verified by immunoprecipi­
tation (Fig. 5B). Prediction based on the phosphorylation database 
revealed that FAK may Tyr-phosphorylate NFATc1 at the Y270 site, 
which is a conserved site of protein sequences among different species 
(Fig. 5C and D). Y15, a specific inhibitor of FAK, significantly decreased 
the phosphorylation of NFATc1 (Fig. 5E). We constructed a dephos­
phorylation mutation of NFATc1 at Y270 site. The dephosphorylation 
mutation Y270F is hardly phosphorylated by FAK (Fig. 5E). These re­
sults suggested that FAK phosphorylated NFATc1 at the Y270 site. 
Previous results indicated that CML mediated the crosstalk between the 
acetylation and phosphorylation of NFATc1 via SIRT3. Interestingly, we 
also found that FAK was involved in the crosstalk. Compared with the 
CML group, the phosphorylation of NFATc1 was further inhibited and 
the acetylation of NFATc1 was further elevated in the CML + Y15 group 
(Fig. 5F). Under the regulation of SIRT3 or FAK, phosphorylation and 
acetylation of NFATc1 completely changed in the opposite direction, 
suggesting that the acetylation of NFATc1 at K549 site and the phos­
phorylation at Y270 site may be antagonistic to each other. Since CML 
had little effects on the P-FAKTyr397 level (Fig. 5F), we inferred that CML 
promoted NFATc1 acetylation by down-regulating SIRT3, which 
inhibited the phosphorylation of NFATc1. Further, we investigated the 
changes in the acetylation and phosphorylation of NFATc1 mutation. 
Compared with NFATc1 WT, the phosphorylation of Y270F mutation 
decreased significantly, while the acetylation of Y270F mutation 
increased significantly (Fig. 5G). Also, the K549 mutation hardly un­
derwent acetylation, but its phosphorylation was obvious (Fig. 5G). 
CML-mediated downregulation of SIRT3 broke the balance between 
phosphorylation and acetylation (Fig. 5H). The inhibition of SIRT3 
increased the acetylation of NFATc1 at K549 site, which antagonized the 
phosphorylation of NFATc1 at Y270 site. 
3.6. SIRT3 and FAK inhibit NFATc1 nuclear translocation in VSMCs 
NFATc1 is an important transcription factor involved in multiple 
transcriptional mediations [29,30]. We then explored the effects of FAK 
and SIRT3 on the nuclear translocation of NFATc1 in VSMCs. The sub­
cellular localization of NFATc1 was labeled with immunofluorescence 
staining and observed with confocal microscopy. We found that 
compared with the siNC +
CML group, knockdown of SIRT3 
(siSIRT3+CML group) significantly enhanced NFATc1 nuclear trans­
location (Fig. 6A and B). SIRT3 overexpression attenuated CML-induced 
NFATc1 nuclear translocation (Fig. 6A and B). Compared with the CML 
group, inhibition of FAK with Y15 (CML + Y15 group) significantly 
increased NFATc1 nuclear translocation (Fig. 6A and B). We also found 
that NFATc1 level in nuclear extracts was further elevated after inhibi­
tion of FAK with Y15 or knockdown of SIRT3 with siSIRT3 (Fig. 6C and 
D), while nuclear NFATc1 level was significantly reduced after over­
expression of SIRT3 (Fig. 6D). Nuclear translocation is important for 
transcriptional regulation of NFATc1. A 96-well plate pre-coated with 
NFATc1 common binding sequence was used to test the transcriptional 
activity of NFATc1. CML significantly promoted the transcriptional ac­
tivity of NFATc1, which was further amplified by knockdown of SIRT3 
or inhibition of FAK (Fig. 6E). Overexpression of SIRT3 significantly 
antagonized promotion of NFATc1 transcriptional activity induced by 
CML (Fig. 6E). Therefore, SIRT3 and FAK play an important role in 
regulating CML-induced NFATc1 nuclear translocation and activity. 
Then we used GFP labeled plasmids to characterize the localization of 
NFATc1 and its mutants (Fig. 6F). We found that NFATc1 WT was 
distributed in both cytoplasm and nucleus. NFATc1 Y270F was more 
obviously distributed in the nucleus, while NFATc1 K549R was mostly 
distributed in the cytoplasm.

4. Discussion 
The present study clarified the role of CML in promoting vascular 
calcification in DM. We identified NFATc1 as the novel regulator of 
CML-promoted vascular calcification (Fig. 8). In terms of the mecha­
nism, CML could promote nuclear translocation and transcriptional ac­
tivity of NFATc1, thereby enhancing the osteogenic differentiation of 
VSMCs. CML also mediated the crosstalk between NFATc1 phosphory­
lation and acetylation. The balance of phosphorylation and acetylation 
was maintained by FAK and SIRT3. CML promoted acetylation of 
NFATc1 at K549 via downregulation of SIRT3, which further antago­
nized phosphorylation of NFATc1 at Y270 and increased nuclear 
translocation and transcriptional regulatory activity of NFATc1. This 
study provided new ideas for clinical intervention of vascular calcifi­
cation in DM. 
AGEs are generated in the Maillard reaction after primary reaction 
between aldehydes and amines or amides, and they were involved in 
progression of a variety of diabetic complications [33,34]. Different 
ideas were provided for intervening AGE-related pathogenesis. On the 
one hand, approaches to prevent the formation of AGEs were raised. 
Some methylglyoxal (a potent precursor of AGEs) enzymes and degly­
cosylase were found [35], and AGEs inhibitors were developed [36]. On 
the other hand, some studies intended to block the AGE-related patho­
genic pathways after the receptors and possible molecular targets of 
AGEs were identified [37,38]. However, due to the complexity of AGEs 
components and related signals, the formation of AGEs and their path­
ogenic mechanism are still unclear. CML is the main active ingredient of 
AGEs. Meloche et al. claimed that CML/RAGE/STAT3/Pim1 axis could 
activate NFATc1 and promote VSMC proliferation and decrease 
apoptosis [39]. Our former studies also reported that CML promoted 
vascular calcification via CML/RAGE signaling [3,13,40]. The present 
study further revealed the novel mechanism of CML in promoting 
vascular calcification through NFATc1. We speculate that activation of 
NFATc1 in vascular calcification may be related to CML/RAGE 
signaling. However, VSMC apoptosis is known to be the key in the 
process of vascular calcification [41,42]. Therefore, in the progression of 
vascular calcification in DM, NFATc1 may not be activated through 
STAT3/Pim1, but through the phosphorylation-acetylation crosstalk 
mediated by FAK and SIRT3, as revealed in this present study. 
Vascular calcification is an active process of mineralized crystals 
ectopically deposited in the arterial wall or atherosclerotic plaque. In 
this process, the osteogenic/osteoclastic balance is broken, and VSMCs 
undergo osteogenic phenotype transdifferentiation in response to 
calcification stimulation signals [43]. Meanwhile, the increase in 
pro-calcified factors and the decrease in inhibitory factors further 
accelerate the evolution of calcification [44]. Due to the metabolic 
disorders in DM, the stimulation factors and signals of vascular calcifi­
cation are more complex compared with those in non-diabetic disorders. 
Previous studies have shown that vascular calcification in DM can be 
classified into medial and intimal calcification according to its distri­
bution [2] or macro- and micro-calcification according to its 
morphology [3]. These calcifications occur simultaneously in diabetic 
arteries, but their regulatory mechanisms are markedly different, which 
directly reflects the complexity of diabetic vascular calcification. 
Therefore, we screened the DEGs between vascular calcification in 
diabetic and non-diabetic disorders with RNA-seq of the mouse aorta 
media and analyzed the functional enrichment based on DEGs results. 
Since AGEs were previously confirmed to promote calcification, we 
mainly focused on the DEGs in the KEGG ‘AGE-RAGE signaling pathway 
in diabetic complications’. Combined with previous studies and our 
validation experiments, we identified the key regulatory role of NFATc1 
in promoting vascular calcification in DM. 
The NFAT family was first identified in T cells, and it is associated 
with T cell antigen receptor signaling [45]. The NFAT family had 5 
isoforms (NFAT1-5) [46], and NFATc1 (also known as NFAT2) was a key 
regulator in the terminal differentiation of osteoclasts [47]. Meanwhile, 
Goettsch et al. reported that the NFATc1 signal could also promote 
osteogenic differentiation of VSMCs [48]. Therefore, NFATc1 is a unique 
molecule in the switch between osteoclast differentiation and osteogenic 
differentiation. Interestingly, Goettsch et al. found that OM promoted 
VSMCs independent of NFATc1. We also found that knockdown of 
NFATc1 in cells/mice could not completely eliminate calcification, 
which may be due to the effects of OM on VSMCs. The role of NFATc1 in 
the regulation of the osteogenesis-osteoclastic balance attracted our 
attention and we also verified the up regulation of NFATc1 in human 
anterior tibial arteries. Since we only collected 19 patients, the number 
of clinical samples is worthy of further expansion to explore the true role 
of NFATc1. It is known that osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis are two 
opposite processes. Osteoclasts are the only cells with bone resorption

Protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation are regulated 
through phosphokinase and phosphatase. Phosphorylation mainly af­
fects the protein activity and subcellular localization, which further 
mediates intracellular signal transmission [50]. The most common 
phosphorylated amino acids are Ser, Thr and Tyr. Previous studies have 
reported the Ser phosphorylation of NFATc1 [26,51], but in this study, 
only Tyr phosphorylation of NFATc1 was changed significantly under 
CML stimulation, rather than Ser/Thr phosphorylation. Then we found 
novel phosphorylation of NFATc1 at Y270 with phosphorylation pre­
diction and site mutation verification. The NFATc1 protein sequence 
contains a nuclear localization signal K-R-K at 267-269 positions. 
Phosphorylation at the Y270 site may affect the nuclear localization 
signal and inhibit nuclear translocation of NFATc1. The tyrosine kinase 
FAK plays a key role in mediating cell adhesion and migration, and it has 
been proved to promote the migration and proliferation of VSMCs [52, 
53]. Our previous studies also showed that CML could activate FAK in 
foam cells [54]. However, in this study, we found that CML had no 
significant effect on the FAK activity in VSMCs under the condition of 
osteogenic induction. It suggested that CML + OM may not influence 
VSMC proliferation and migration. It may also be that the activation of 
FAK by CML counteracts the inhibition of FAK during osteogenic dif­
ferentiation and apoptosis of VSMCs. 
Protein acetylation mainly occurs at lysine sites, which affects pro­
tein stability and expression [55]. In this study, we found that NFATc1 
acetylation was significantly increased by CML; hence we focused on the 
deacetylase of NFATc1 and found that SIRT3 played a key role in 
regulating NFATc1 acetylation. In fact, protein acetylation is not only 
regulated by deacetylase, but it is also affected by acetylase. Kim et al. 
reported that the p300/cyclic adenosine monophosphate-response ele­
ment-binding protein-associated factor could acetylate NFATc1 and in­
crease its stability [56]. More studies are needed to confirm whether 
acetylase is involved in the process of CML-induced NFATc1 acetylation. 
In addition, the study by Kim et al. also revealed that HDAC5 could 
deacetylate NFATc1 [56]. In our study, compared with NAM (a SIRT 
inhibitor), TSA (an HDAC inhibitor) +NAM significantly increased 
acetylation of NFATc1, suggesting that HDAC may also be the deace­
tylase of NFATc1 in addition to SIRT, which is consistent with the study 
by Kim et al. More importantly, we observed that compared with the 
addition of TSA, TSA + CML significantly increased acetylation of 
NFATc1. Also, compared with NAM, NAM + CML did not significantly 
affect acetylation of NFATc1. Therefore, we speculate that CML regu­
lates acetylation of NFATc1 through the SIRT family. In addition to 
SIRT3, other members of the SIRT family play important roles in the 
regulation of cardiovascular disease [57–59]. Chen et al. reported that 
SIRT1 had a protective effect on abdominal aortic aneurysm [57]. Liu 
et al. found that knockdown of SIRT1 in chronic kidney disease models 
facilitated vascular calcification [58]. In our study, we found that CML 
down-regulated SIRT1 and SIRT3. SIRT1 had no significant effect on 
acetylation of NFATc1 (data not shown). SIRT3 promoted diabetic 
vascular calcification by deacetylating NFATc1. Whether SIRT1 also 
plays a role in CML-induced vascular calcification needs further study. 
Protein post-translational modification crosstalk is a critical way of 
protein function regulation [60]. The phosphorylation-acetylation 
crosstalk has also been reported in previous studies. Zhu et al. found 
that dephosphorylation of malic enzyme 1 (ME1) by the phosphoglyc­
erate mutase 5 increased the acetylation of ME1 by the acetyl-CoA 
acetyltransferase 1, thereby affecting lipogenesis and colorectal tumor­
igenesis [28]. The present study also found that NFATc1 phosphoryla­
tion and acetylation are antagonistic. Increased acetylation of NFATc1 
inhibited its phosphorylation. In the study by Yang et al., they found that 
the acetylation of Myristoylated Alanine-Rich C Kinase Substrate pro­
moted its phosphorylation [61]. Therefore, acetylation and phosphor­
ylation may be antagonistic or synergistic. In addition to the 
phosphorylation-acetylation 
crosstalk, 
we 
also 
noticed 
ubiquitin-acetylation crosstalk. Protein acetylation can affect its ubiq­
uitination, and then regulate protein expression through the ubiquiti­
nation proteasome system [62,63]. In our study, CML promotes NFATc1 
expression and acetylation. In our future studies. we will further 
investigate whether CML affects its expression through NFATc1 acety­
lation and ubiquitination crosstalk. 
Our study reported a novel molecular mechanism in CML-induced 
vascular calcification in DM. CML significantly increased the activity 
of NFATc1 via affecting acetylation-phosphorylation crosstalk. CML 
increased the acetylation of NFATc1 at K549 via downregulating SIRT3, 
thus antagonizing phosphorylation of NFATc1 Y270 by FAK. The 
increased acetylation of NFATc1 mediated stabilized its function and 
transcriptional regulatory activity. Our study provides clues for a better 
understanding of vascular calcification in DM and ideas for the devel­
opment of therapeutic targets.
