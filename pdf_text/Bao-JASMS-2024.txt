ABSTRACT: Protein lactylation is a novel post-translational modification (PTM)
involved in many important physiological processes such as macrophage polarization,
immune regulation, and tumor cell growth. However, traditional methodologies for
studying lactylation have predominantly relied on peptide enrichment from whole-cell
lysates, which tend to favor the detection of high-abundance peptides, thus limiting
the identification of low-abundance lactylated peptides. To address this limitation,
here, we employed subcellular fractionation to separate proteins and map lactylated
peptides from each isolated subcellular fraction using a model cell line. In brief, we
identified 1,217 lysine lactylation (Kla) sites on 553 proteins across four subcellular
fractions. Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis revealed that Kla proteins
participate in distinct pathways depending on the subcellular contexts. In addition,
this subcellular fractionation method enabled the discovery of 36 previously
unreported Kla proteins and 223 novel Kla sites, many of which are present in low
abundance. Notably, several proteins contain multiple newly identified Kla sites, exemplified by the transcriptional regulator ATRX.
Furthermore, our results indicate the possibility of PTM crosstalk between Kla and other PTMs such as ubiquitination and
sumoylation. In conclusion, subcellular fractionation facilitates the identification of Kla proteins that have been previously uncovered
and could be overlooked by affinity enrichment of whole-cell lysates.
KEYWORDS: Lactylation, subcellular fractionation, PTM, proteomics
■INTRODUCTION
Kla is a recently discovered PTM that occurs on proteins. Kla
has emerged as a crucial epigenetic regulator in diverse cellular
processes, including the modulation of gene transcription,1,2
reprogramming of tumor metabolism,3,4 inflammation5,6 and
macrophage polarization.7 Initially, lactylation was found to be
primarily localized to nuclear histones. Subsequently, lactyla-
tion on cytosol-localized metabolic enzymes were discov-
ered.8−10 Then, an expanding body of research have indicated
that Kla widely occurs on diverse substrates in various types of
cells and thereby modulates various physiological and
pathological processes.11−14
At present, antibody-based enrichment coupled with mass
spectrometry stands as the primary method for identifying and
quantifying Kla peptides and sites. Nevertheless, mapping Kla
sites with such classic methods are often hindered by its biased
detection toward high-abundance proteins, exemplified by
histones in nuclei and fructose-bisphosphate aldolase A
(ALDOA),9 adenosine kinase 2 (AK2)8 and high mobility
group protein B1 (HMGB1)5 in cytosol. Therefore, how to
detect low-to-medium abundance Kla peptides remains
challenging. It is worthy of note that researchers have
employed subcellular fractionation strategies to isolate proteins
in subcellular organelles or regions and then perform
proteomic analysis for modified peptides. This has been
successfully practices for PTMs including acetylation,15,16
phosphorylation17−20 and glycosylation.21 This improvement
has been found to significantly increase the detection coverage,
and the newly identified PTM sites include low-abundance
proteins.
Therefore, we proposed to first isolate major subcellular
compartments from cells, including cytoplasm, nuclei,
mitochondria, and membrane, and then perform the classic
affinity enrichment analysis for each fraction. The proposed
subcellular mapping workflow holds the potential to isolate the
high-abundant lactylated proteins, such as histones and
ALDOA, from the repertoire of Kla peptides and may allow
discovery of novel Kla peptides, especially from proteins
expressed at low-to-medium abundance in cells. In addition,
data-independent acquisition (DIA) has been widely used to
analyze hundreds of phosphoproteomes with improved
coverage and sensitivity.22,23 Therefore, to increase the
detection coverage, both data-dependent acquisition (DDA)
and DIA modes were employed for Kla mapping from each
isolated fraction. In summary, subcellular lactylproteome
analysis allows unveiling 223 Kla sites and 36 lactylated
proteins that have not been reported as lactylated substrates in

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Cell Culture and Chemicals. The human colon
carcinoma HCT116 cells were purchased from Cell Bank/
Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were
cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640
medium medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented
with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, Beit
Haemek, Israel) and 100 units/mL penicillin and streptomycin
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.
LC-MS grade water and acetonitrile (ACN) were obtained
from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Sequencing-grade trypsin
were purchased from Beijing Shengxia Proteins Scientific Ltd.
(Beijing, China). Unless otherwise stated, all other chemicals
were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.
Protein Extraction and Subcellular Separation. Cells
were treated with 25 mM sodium lactate (NaLac) for 48 h and
then harvested following by washing three times with cold
phosphate buffer saline (PBS). The cell pellets were
centrifuged at 800 g for 5 min and then lysed in RIPA lysis
buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat. no. P1003B) containing
protease and phosphatase inhibitors (ApexBio Technology,
cat. no. K1008 and K1013) on ice for 30 min. Subcellular
components were extracted using three commercial kits
according to the manufacturer’s procedures. Specifically, the
Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kits (Beyotime
Biotechnology, cat. no. P0027) were used for the collection of
nuclear proteins. The Mitochondria/Nuclei Isolation Kit
(KeyGEN BioTECH, cat. no. KGA828) was used for
cytoplasmic and mitochondrial proteins. The Minute Plasma
Membrane Protein Isolation Kit (Invent Biotechnologies,
catalog no. SM-005) was used for membranal proteins. The
protein concentrations were determined by the bicinchoninic
acid (BCA) assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat. no. P0011).
Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining. HCT116 cells were
seeded on coverslips in 12-well plates and treated with either
the solvent or 25 mM NaLac individually for 48 h. Following
the treatment, the cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with
ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized using
0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 10% donkey
serum for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells
were incubated with primary antibodies against Kla (1:1000,
PTM Bio Inc., cat. no. PTM-1401RM), β-actin (1:200,
Proteintech, cat. no. 66009-1-Ig) and 60 kDa heat shock
protein (HSP60; 1:200, Proteintech, cat. no. 66041−1-Ig) in
2% donkey serum at 4 °C overnight. Cells were subsequently
washed five times with PBS and incubated with Donkey Anti-
Mouse IgG (H+L) Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, Abcam,
cat. no. AB150105) and Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L)
Antibody, Alexa Fluor 647 (1:500, Abcam, catalog no.
AB150075), respectively, for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the
cells were incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole
(DAPI; Abcam, cat. no. AB104139) for 5 min at room
temperature to facilitate staining. The images of stained cells
were captured using an LSM700 confocal microscope (Zeiss,
Oberkochen, Germany).
Immunoblotting. Briefly, the lysates were diluted by 5 ×
loading buffer (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, cat. no.
WB2001), heated to 100 °C for 10 min, cooled and separated
on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electro-
phoresis (SDS−PAGE) gels. Proteins were then transferred
onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes, blocked
with 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1%
Tween 20 detergent (TBST), and incubated with primary
antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were
subsequently washed five times with TBST and incubated
with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary
antibody for 1 h at 37 °C. The immunoblotted bands were
captured on a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Biorad, Hercules,
USA) and analyzed by Image Lab software. The primary
antibodies employed in this study included the antibodies
against glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH;
1:5000, Proteintech, cat. no. 60004−1-Ig), Lamin B1 (1:5000,
Proteintech, cat. no. 12987−1-AP), Cytochrome c oxidase
subunit 4 isoform 1 (COX IV; 1:5000, Proteintech, cat. no.
11242−1-AP), sodium/potassium-transporting ATPase sub-
unit alpha-1 (ATP1A1; 1:5000, Proteintech, cat. no. 14418−1-
AP) and Kla (1:1000, PTM Bio Inc., cat. no. PTM-1401RM).
Proteomic Sample Preparation. To remove the
detergent in lysis buffer, methanol, chloroform, and water
were added to the lysate according to the ratio 4:1:3:1 by
volume followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min to
precipitate the proteome. The flaky precipitate was washed
twice with methanol and resolubilized by 8 M urea in 25 mM
ammonium bicarbonate solution. Proteins were then reduced
by 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 56 °C for 30 min and
alkylated by 40 mM iodoacetamide (IAM) at room temper-
ature in the dark for 20 min. Additional DTT was added and
allowed to react with excess IAM at room temperature for 10
min. The mixtures were added with 25 mM ammonium
bicarbonate to dilute urea to 1 M followed by overnight
digestion with sequencing-grade trypsin at an enzyme/protein
ratio of 1:50 (w/w) at 37 °C. The digestion was quenched by
adding formic acid (FA). The resultant digests were desalted
with Sep-Pak C18 cartridges (Waters, cat. no. WAT054955)
and evaporated to dryness with a vacuum centrifuge (Thermo
Fisher Scientific). The samples were stored at −80 °C until
further proteomic analysis.
High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)
Fractionation. The proteome was dissolved in HPLC phase
A buffer (10 mM ammonium formate containing 5% ACN)
and then fractionated on a Shimadzu Nexera XR liquid
chromatography system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Phase B
consisted of ACN with 20% 10 mM ammonium formate in
aqueous buffer. The flow rate was set as 0.2 mL/min, and the
gradient was set as follows: 0−79 min, 5−50% B; 79−81 min,
50−100% B; 81−98 min, 100% B; 98−100 min, 100−5% B;
100−120 min, 5% B. The effluent was collected every 1.5 min,
yielding a total of 48 fractions. The fractions were then pooled
in intervals to ensure that the peptide content of each pool was
approximately 0.5 mg. The lyophilized fractions were dissolved
in 0.1% FA, desalted with Sep-Pak C18 cartridges and stored at
−80 °C before analysis.
Affinity Enrichment of Lactylated Peptides. Briefly,
protein A/G plus agarose beads (Santa, cat. no. sc-2003) were
washed with ETN buffer (1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH
8.0) and 100 mM NaCl) three times, blocked with 5% BSA
and then conjugated with anti-Kla antibody (PTM Bio Inc.,
cat. no. PTM-1401RM) by incubation at 4 °C overnight.
Then, the trypsin-digested peptides were incubated with the
prepared antibody-conjugated beads for 6 h at 4 °C with rotary
shaking. The beads were washed three times with ETN buffer,

Proteomic Analysis by LC−MS/MS. The digested
peptides were analyzed by an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass
spectrometer equipped with an EASY-nano LC 1200 system
(Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phase A consisting of 0.1%
FA in water and B consisting of ACN-H2O (8:2, v/v) were
delivered at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Peptides were analyzed
using a 120 min chromatography gradient from 3 to 38% phase
B during 0−102 min on an Acclaim PepMap RSLC column
(75 μm × 250 mm, Thermo Fisher Scientific). During DDA
analysis, MS1 spectra were collected at the m/z range of 350−
1,800 at a resolution of 120,000 on the Orbitrap with a
maximum AGC value of 4e5. For MS2 acquisition,
fragmentation was conducted by HCD with the CE value set
at 32% after optimization. MS2 spectra were collected with the
first mass set at m/z 100 at a resolution of 30,000 on the
Orbitrap with a maximum AGC of 5e4. Besides, for DIA
analysis, variable acquisition windows (n = 60) were applied to
the MS2 acquisition, and the m/z range was determined to be
350−1,800.
Proteomic Database Searching. The collected data for
DDA analysis were searched using PEAKS Studio XPro
(Bioinformatics Solutions) against the Uniprot human data-
base (UniProt_Human_reviewed_15−05−2023. fasta). A
mass tolerance of 10 ppm was allowed for precursor ions,
and 0.02 Da was allowed for fragment ions. Other parameters
used for data analysis include trypsin as the protease, allowing
a maximum of three missed cleavages and semispecific
digestion. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine (+57.02 Da)
was set as fixed modification, and methionine oxidation
(+15.99 Da), acetylation at protein N termini (+42.02 Da),
and lysine lactylation (+72.02 Da) were set as variable
modifications. Peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) were filtered
to a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) employing a target-decoy
database search approach. For lactylation assignment, the
modified peptides must reach an AScore ≥20.9,24
The raw DIA data were searched against the human
database using Spectronaut (version 19, Biognosys AG,
Schliren, Switzerland) in library-free mode (directDIA). The
settings for digestion and modification were identical to those
used in PEAKS Studio. PTM localization filter was checked,
and the PTM localization cutoff was set to 0.75. Unless
otherwise stated, other parameters were used with default
settings.
Bioinformatics. Lactylated proteins mined from the
affinity-enriched lactylproteome were subjected to Gene
Ontology (biological process) functional annotation by
DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8 (https://David.ncifcrf.
gov/). The protein localization was obtained from the Gene
Ontology (cellular component) annotation in UniProt
(https://www.uniprot.org/) with the subcellular fractions
“cytoplasm”, “nuclei”, “mitochondria”, and “membrane”
classified according to the cellular component category, as
detailed in Supporting Information (SI) Table S1. Addition-
ally, four classical post-translationally modified substrate
proteins were retrieved from the iPTMnet database (https://
research.bioinformatics.udel.edu/iptmnet/). The motif analysis
was conducted using WEBLOGE (https://weblogo.berkeley.
edu/logo.cgi). To perform the KEGG pathway annotation
network and pathway enrichment analysis, we utilized the
ClueGO application in Cytoscape25 (version 3.7.1) and the
Metascape platform26 (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html),
respectively. Filtering criteria included group P value <0.05 and
the inclusion of at least three genes in each group.
Statistical Analysis. Statistical analysis was performed in
Prism v.8.0.1. Generally, data represent the mean ± s.e.m. The
statistical significance of differences in protein abundance and
pPSE values between two groups (data points were not
normally distributed) was determined using two-tailed Mann−
Whitney U tests, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Mapping Subcellular Localization for Proteins Bear-
ing Classical PTMs. PTMs can significantly influence the
modified proteins’ function, stability, and intracellular local-
ization.27−29 Currently, the landscape of novel Kla sites is
actively expanding, and this knowledge serves as the key to
uncover their functional roles. To increase the detectable
coverage for Kla, we asked whether Kla mapping has been
hindered by the proteomic bias toward high-abundance
modified proteins. We first analyzed four classical protein
lysine modifications (PLMs), including methylation, acetyla-
tion, ubiquitination and sumoylation, retrieved from the
iPTMnet database, and then performed Gene Ontology
(GO) Cellular Component (CC) analysis to classify the
modified proteins based on their subcellular localizations
(Figure 1A). The category used for CC annotation was
summarized in Table S1. We analyzed four major subcellular
categories, including the cytoplasm, nuclei, mitochondria, and
membrane (referring to plasma membrane in this study). The

Besides classic PLMs, we next asked whether the biased
detection of modified peptides in the cytoplasm holds true for
relatively new acylations. We compiled existing literature on
lysine crotonylation (Kcr), succinylation (Ksucc) and Kla, and
created a library for reported PLM sites and proteins (Table
S2).1,2,4,8,9,38−45 Again, we found that the acylated proteins are
mostly localized in cytoplasm and second enriched in nuclei,
which was consistent with the more common PLMs shown in
Figure 1B. Together, such uneven localization patterns for
modified peptides suggest that separating subcellular compart-
ments and then performing affinity enrichment for each
fraction may remove the high-abundance modified peptides
and improve the detection coverage.
Immunofluorescence Imaging of Lactylated Proteins
Inside Cells. Interestingly, lactylation shows significant
enrichment in nuclei relative to other acylations. This agrees
to its initial detection in histones in macrophages and the
extensive, continuous reports on histones in many other cells
and organisms.1,11,46 In agreement, we performed IF experi-
ments for HCT116 cells administered with NaLac, and we
observed concentrated Kla signals in nuclei. Nevertheless, IF
images of lactylated proteins also showed colocalization with β-
actin (Figure 2A) and HSP60 (Figure 2B). Thus, these
phenomena indicate that lactylated proteins are also
distributed in the cytoplasm and mitochondria, although at
much lower intensities compared to the nuclei. Thus, together
with CC analysis of previously reported Kla sites (Figure 1B),
we propose that, if we isolate subcellular compartments and
then perform affinity enrichment-based lactylproteome anal-
ysis, we may circumvent the biased detection toward abundant
lactylated peptides and uncover lactylated peptides of low
abundances. This has been demonstrated to be successful in
expanding the coverage of acetylated,15,16 phosphorylated17−20
and glycosylated21 peptides.
Characterizing the Subcellular Lactylproteome of
Model Cells. To characterize the lactylated peptides in
different subcellular locations, we isolated individual fractions
of cytoplasm, nuclei, membrane, and mitochondria using the
model cells, HCT116 cells, and assessed the purity of the
extracted subcellular fractions. Successful isolation of cyto-
plasm, nuclei, membrane, and mitochondria was verified by
immunoblotting against marker proteins specifically expressed
in subcellular organelles (Figure 3A). Then, we immuno-
blotted the whole-cell proteins and lactylated proteins present
in the isolated subcellular fractions using pan-Kla antibody,
confirming that different expression patterns were observed for
each fraction (Figure S1A, Figure 3B). This supports our
hypothesis that separating the different compartments may aid
in identifying distinct Kla sites and proteins. Thus, we

Following such a workflow, we analyzed the lactylproteome
of HCT116 cells. We found that DIA-based Kla mapping
identified 1,010 Kla sites from 482 proteins and DDA-based
detection identified 669 Kla sites from 366 proteins (Figure
3D). A significant overlap was observed between the DDA and
DIA-detected Kla in both peptides and sites (82% for the
DDA-identified Kla peptides and 56% for the DIA-identified
Kla peptides; 83% for the DDA-identified Kla sites and 55% for
the DIA-identified Kla sites) (Figure S1B,C). Therefore, we
combined both the DDA and DIA-detected Kla peptides and
constructed a Kla peptide pool consisting of 1,262 Kla peptides
and 1,217 Kla sites corresponding to 553 proteins for further
analysis (Table S3). It is noteworthy that C-terminal
modifications, specifically on lysine (K) or arginine (R)
residues, are generally believed to inhibit trypsin activity,
preventing cleavage.47,48 As a result, modified peptides that are
cleaved are often regarded as false positives and are typically
removed from the analysis. In our study, we excluded C-
terminal modified peptides from the statistics of Kla peptides,
which accounted for approximately 1.4% of the total modified
peptide population. Notably, by comparing with the Kla sites
identified by affinity enrichment analysis of whole-cell lysates,
the subcellular lactylproteome method identified ∼3 fold of
Kla sites (Figure 3D). Additionally, we found the majority of
1,217 Kla sites are situated on the modified protein surface,
exhibiting lower pPSE (prediction-aware part-sphere exposure)
values compared to the unmodified lysine reisudes retrived
from the same subset of proteins (Figure S1D). The pPSE
value reflects the number of proximal alpha-carbons within a
conical volume extending 12 Å along the Cα-Cβ vector with an
internal angle of 70°. A high pPSE value indicates that a
residue is more buried and less accessible, while a low pPSE
value signifies greater accessibility.49
For the collected subcellular lactylproteome, different
numbers of Kla sites were identified among the four subcellular
fractions: cytoplasm (342 Kla sites from 189 proteins), nuclei
(1,064 Kla sites from 449 proteins), mitochondria (91 Kla sites
from 74 proteins), and membrane (95 Kla sites from 66
proteins) (Figure 4A). Sequence motif analysis shows that
motifs of Kla peptides in distinct subcellular compartment also
differ (Figure S1E). An interesting case is the motif for Kla
peptides localized in nuclear fractions is enriched with
positively charged K and R, which may be related to rich-
Lys/Arg chromatin proteins. Additionally, the motifs for Kla
peptides from membrane proteins are enriched with hydro-
phobic residues such as Met and Ala, which agrees with the
biophysical properties of membrane proteins. These differ-
ences indicate that Kla modifications are installed on proteins
localized to different subcellular compartments and biophyscial
contexts.

To compare the differences in the pathways of Kla proteins

lactylated proteins are enriched in RNA metabolism,
chromatin remodeling, and DNA repair processes. In the

chromatin remodeling, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis,
and transcription regulation. In mitochondria, lactylated
proteins are enriched in cellular signaling, immune responses,
and metabolic processes. In the membrane, lactylated proteins
show enrichment in pathways related to senescence and
autophagy. These findings suggest that lactylation may play
diverse regulatory roles, depending on cellular compartments,
potentially influencing gene expression, RNA processing, stress
responses, and metabolic regulation in a highly localized
manner.
Discovery of Novel Kla Proteins and Functional
Insights from Newly Identified Kla Sites. Based on the
collected subcellular lactylproteome, we sought to verify our
hypothesis that subcellular fractionation aids in identifying
novel and possibly low-to-medium abundance proteins
carrying Kla. First, we compiled reported Kla sites in the
literature to a large public Kla library consisting of 15,850 Kla
sites and 4,385 modified proteins summarized by our lab
(Table S2). Comparison of our subcellular lactylproteome led
us to find that, surprisingly, although the public Kla library
includes significantly more Kla sites than our collected
lactylproteome, 223 Kla sites and 36 Kla proteins are identified
only through our subcellular fractionation method (Figure
5A). The pathway enrichment analysis of 36 newly identified
Kla proteins highlights their involvement in many key cellular
processes including the regulation of TP53 activity and p53-
mediated signal transduction, suggesting a role for lactylation
in stress response and tumor suppression. Additionally,
lactylation is also linked to RNA metabolism, ribonucleopro-
tein complex biogenesis, and RNA polymerase II transcription,
implying its impact on gene expression and RNA processing.
Other processes such as protein localization to the
chromosome, nuclear migration, and cell development are
also affected, indicating that the newly identified lactylation
proteins play a broad regulatory role across different cellular

Analyzing the intensities and rank of the abundances for the

Intriguingly, analysis of the number of Kla sites carried by
each modified protein showed that approximately 58.6%
proteins only carry one Kla peptide, while 1.8% proteins
carry more than ten Kla peptides (Figure 6A). We even found
proteins that carry more than ten Kla peptides, and seven of
them carry at least one newly identified Kla peptide (Figure
6B). As shown in the Nightingale rose chart, a transcriptional
regulator ATRX (ATRX) is the most notable, as it carries
seven known Kla sites including K307, K568, K690, K715,
K768, K805, and K830 and three newly identified Kla sites
including K438, K584, and K1099 by our approach (Figure
6C). ATRX is crucial in the regulation of chromatin state, gene
expression and the DNA damage response.53 It has been
demonstrated that ATRX predominantly localizes to hetero-
chromatic regions via its interaction with heterochromatin
protein 1 homologue alpha (HP1α). Interestingly, K584 of
ATRX, one of the newly identified Kla sites, is located within
the conserved PxVxL motif that is responsible for the
interaction with HP1α.54 This elicits an intriguing speculation
that Kla at K584 may affect the interaction between ATRX and
HP1α and thereby lead to changed localization at the
heterochromatin of ATRX.
PTM Analysis Reveals the Crosstalk between Lacty-
lation and Other PLMs. Lastly, we investigated the potential
crosstalk between lactylation and other PLMs including
ubiquitination, acetylation, sumoylation, and methylation. We
searched the iPTMnet database for the presence of other
PLMs on the 1,217 Kla sites identified in this study. Our
finding indicates that Kla sites significantly overlap with those
reported to bear ubiquitination and acetylation when
compared to those carrying other PLMs. Specifically, 52.0%
of Kla sites have been identified as ubiquitination sites, while
36.7% of Kla sites have been identified as acetylation sites
(Figure 7A). This implies a possible interplay between
lactylation and other PLMs. We focused on the heavily
lactylated proteins to explore the colocalization of other PLMs
on these proteins (Figure 7B). Indeed, we found the lactylated
sites of the nuclear factor interleukin-3-regulated protein
(NFIL3) carry multiple other PLMs. The Kla sites in
NFIL3, including two known Kla sites (K419 and K448)
and three newly identified Kla sites (K71, K360, and K406). It
is noteworthy that some of Kla sites in NFIL3 can also carry
ubiquitination or sumoylation (Figure 7C). NFIL3 is a basic
leucine zipper transcriptional regulator that possesses a
distinctive transcriptional repression domain, spanning amino
acids 299 to 363.55 One of its newly identified Kla sites, K360,
is located in the transcriptional repression domain and is also
essential for its interaction with TATA-box-binding protein
(TBP) -binding repressor protein Dr1.56 Thus, we postulated
that PTMs occurring at this specific site may affect the
transcriptional repressor activity of NFIL3.

facilitates comprehensive characterization of Kla proteins.
