ABSTRACT 
Post Translational Modifications significantly influence conformational preferences and 
activity of proteins. In Protein Kinase-B (Akt2), numerous residues undergo phosphoryl­
ation and O-GlcNAcylation, in same region. This results in a crosstalk mechanism mod­
ulating Akt2 activity, and is dysregulated in metabolic diseases such as type-II diabetes. 
However, atomic level details of how such modifications influence Akt2 dynamics and 
activity is poorly understood. In this work, we employed in silico approaches to explore 
the conformational preferences and functional consequences of Akt2 in O- 
GlcNAcylated, phosphorylated and unmodified states. Multi-microsecond simulations, 
resulting in total sampling of 7.6ls were performed on Akt2 in all the above states, 
and the structural effects of such modifications were analyzed. We found that such 
diverse modifications result in considerable difference in Akt2 dynamics. In phosphory­
lated state, the activation loop of Akt2 is highly flexible and has high solvent accessibil­
ity, in contrast with the buried nature of O-GlcNAcylated Akt2. This involved contacts 
between the O-GlcNAc residues and the pleckstrein homology domain, resulting in its 
burial. In the phosphorylated state, an elaborate hydrogen bonded network was 
observed, where phosphorylated threonine residues and active site residues formed 
hydrogen bonds with the phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3) binding resi­
dues, forming the structural basis of autoinhibition and phosphatase shielding by Akt2. 
We further sampled for first time, the conformational changes associated with the PIP3 
induced Akt2 activation from the Akt2 simulations in presence of PIP3, ATP and Mg2þ
cofactor. This work demonstrated the structural effects of varied post-translational 
modifications, which form a crosstalk mechanism regulating Akt2 activity.

Introduction
Post-Translational Modifications (PTMs) are among the most important determinants of protein structure 
and activity. Though the advent of ab initio methods such as Alphafold3 (Abramson et al., 2024) and 
Rosettafold (Baek et al., 2021) has heralded a revolution in the problem of protein structure, it is clear that 
structure alone is not enough to obtain a complete understanding of their functioning. PTMs, the chemical 
modifications that occur to the protein play significant roles in ensuring correct folding of the protein and 
enabling its functional activity. Yet for various reasons, how PTMs influence the protein structure, dynam­
ics, activity and ultimately the biological processes associated with them remains poorly understood.
Glycosylation is one such PTM that is associated with various physiological processes such as cell sig­
naling, immune responses, cell adhesion to name a few (M. He et al., 2024). It is also associated with 
numerous pathologies such as infectious diseases, cancers and autoimmune diseases (M. He et al., 2024; 
Zhang & Qu, 2021). Though glycosylation primarily occurs with the proteins localized in the extracellular 
space, another form of glycosylation occurs on numerous cytosolic proteins. O-GlcNAc is the 
O-Glycosylation where certain serine and threonine residues are covalently attached with the carbohy­
drate N-acetylglucosamine. Numerous cytosolic proteins including kinases, enzymes and transcription

factors undergo this modification (Wulff-Fuentes et al., 2021). Protein Kinase-B (Akt2) is one such cell sig­
naling protein which undergoes O-GlcNAcylation. Using mass-spectrometry based methods, numerous 
studies have demonstrated that numerous residues in Akt2 undergo O-GlcNAcylation (Risso et al., 2015; 
S. Wang et al., 2012; X. Yang et al., 2008). But interestingly, many of the O-GlcNAcylated residues are 
also phosphorylated.
In numerous proteins, O-GlcNAcylation is considered to be a crosstalk mechanism where a protein 
may be ‘switched on’ with phosphorylation, and it may be ‘switched off’ with O-GlcNAcylation on the 
same site, or on neighboring residues (Soesanto et al., 2008). Many proteins are regulated through this 
mechanism, such as Estrogen receptor-b, p53, c-Myc to name a few (Bond & Hanover, 2015; Garofalo 
et al., 2003; S. Wang et al., 2012). In Akt2, O-GlcNAcylation results in the loss of its activity, and prevents 
its phosphorylation (Soesanto et al., 2008). While Akt and its variants are well studied in context to its 
role in cell proliferation and cancer (Islam et al., 2023), Akt2, the Akt variant expressed in liver and adi­
pose tissues has an important role in the maintenance of glucose homeostasis (Sharma & Dey, 2021). 
Akt2 is part of the insulin signaling cascade, where insulin binding and activation of insulin receptor 
leads to phosphorylation of Akt2. Following its phosphorylation, Akt2 is activated and it further phos­
phorylates transcription factors such as Foxa-2, ultimately leading to upregulation of glucose import 
machinery such as GLUT4, enzymes involved in glycolysis and downregulation of enzymes involved in 
gluconeogenesis (Gonzalez & McGraw, 2009; L. He et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2003; Wolfrum et al., 2003). In 
hyperglycemia, a manifestation of type-II diabetes, Akt2 is found to be O-GlcNAcylated. This modification 
is observed to contribute to insulin resistance, which is coupled with increased gluconeogenesis and 
reduced glucose uptake (Park et al., 2005; Soesanto et al., 2008). Knockdown of O-GlcNAc transferase, 
the enzyme that attaches N-acetylglucosamine to the serine and threonine residues of various proteins 
reversed the hyperglycemia (Soesanto et al., 2008).
Though O-GlcNAc and its cross talk with phosphorylation is observed in various proteins, how each 
of the modification influences the protein conformational preferences has been unexplored so far. 
Mapping the molecular basis of this cross talk in Akt2 will aid in discovery of potential therapeutic 
agents that may reverse the effects associated with Akt2 O-GlcNAcylation. Numerous experimental stud­
ies in past have attempted to map the structural effects of both the modifications at the local level, 
though some of the observations are contradictory. These studies show that both O-GlcNAcylation and 
phosphorylation induce conformational effects that modulate the local secondary structures. 
Experimental studies on estrogen receptor beta (ERb) using nuclear magnetic resonance, circular dichro­
ism spectroscopy and molecular dynamics simulations showed that phosphorylated ERb peptides are 
disordered with no well-defined secondary structure. O-GlcNAcylation at the same site stabilized the 
peptides and induced conformational order (Chen et al., 2006). Similarly, in the microtubule-associated 
tau, hyperphosphorylation is considered to cause aggregation, resulting in the onset of Alzheimer’s dis­
ease through destabilization of local conformation. O-GlcNAc modification on the other hand is shown 
to prevent the same, without compromising its native conformation and microtubule polymerization 
activity (Yuzwa et al., 2014). A similar study on model alpha helical peptides however showed that both 
phosphorylations and O-GlcNAc modifications tend to induce helical conformations compared to 
unmodified peptides (Elbaum & Zondlo, 2014). This stabilization occurred through hydrogen-bonded 
interactions among the side chain groups, as well as through inducing kinks in the local conformation. 
On the other hand, another molecular dynamics based study on model helical peptides showed that 
O-GlcNAcylations and phosphorylations had a stabilizing effect in the N-terminus, but resulted in desta­
bilization in the C-terminus of the peptide (Rani & Mallajosyula, 2021).
While all these studies have attempted to understand the local structural effects of both the modifica­
tions, the effects on the global conformational preferences of the protein remains unexplored till date. 
Similarly, no study so far has attempted to decipher the functional consequences of such modifications 
at the atomic level. In Akt, a previous study attempted to examine the structural effects of O-GlcNAc 
modification on Akt dynamics using molecular modeling and simulations (S. Wang et al., 2012). Though 
this study evaluated differences in Akt2 dynamics between O-GlcNAcylated and unmodified states, the 
crosstalk mechanism arising from O-GlcNAcylation versus phosphorylation modifications were unex­
plored. Thus, in this work, we attempted to address all the above-mentioned lacunae using in silico 
methods.

Materials and methods
Modeling O-GlcNAcylated and phosphorylated Akt2
Experimental X-ray crystallographic structures of Akt2 was for construction of multi-template Akt2 model 
(PDB:9C1W,3D0E) (Craven et al., 2025; Heerding et al., 2008). Multi-template modeling was performed 
using Modeller-10.5 (Webb & Sali, 2016). To ensure adequate quality of the modeled structure, quality of 
the model was assessed using ProSA (Wiederstein & Sippl, 2007) and Ramachandran plot was generated 
for the constructed model using Ramplot server, and the number of residues in disallowed regions were 
identified (https://ramplot.in) (M. Kumar & Rathore, 2025). The scores for both the analysis are provided 
in the supplementary Table 1.
Three variants of starting Akt2 systems were modeled: O-GlcNAcylated Akt2, Phosphorylated Akt2 and 
Akt2 without any post-translational modifications. O-GlcNAc residues were modified on the residues T306, 
T313 and S474. O-GlcNAc was added to these positions because previous studies have demonstrated that 
O-GlcNAc modifications to these positions blocks Akt2 phosphorylation (Figure 1) (Risso et al., 2015; S. 
Wang et al., 2012). Similarly, the residues T309 and S474 were modified with phosphorylations, due to the 
fact that phosphorylation of these residues are found to be essential for Akt2 activity (Figure 1) (Risso 
et al., 2015). All the PTMs were modeled using the CHARMM-GUI solution builder (Jo et al., 2008).
Molecular dynamics simulations
For all the simulated systems, CHARMM36m force field was used to describe the protein as well as the 
PTMs (Guvench et al., 2011; Huang et al., 2017). The protein systems were embedded in a cubic box 
with edge distance of 1.0 nm. Following this, the system was solvated with TIP3P model, and 0.1 M of 
NaCl was added for neutralizing the system (Jorgensen et al., 1983). The above-mentioned simulation 
setup steps were performed using CHARMM-GUI solution builder (Jo et al., 2008). Unless mentioned 
otherwise, all the following simulation protocols were performed using GROMACS-2020 (Berendsen 
et al., 1984; Kutzner et al., 2019). The constructed systems were subjected to energy minimization using 
steepest-descent method. For all the following simulation steps, electrostatic interactions were calcu­
lated using PME method, and the bonds involving hydrogens were constrained using LINCS method

(Darden et al., 1993; Hess et al., n.d). Following the minimization, the system was equilibrated at NVT 
ensemble for 1 ns, with timestep of 0.001ps. Position restraints were imposed on all the heavy atoms of 
the protein, and constant temperature was maintained at 310K using v-rescale thermostat (Bussi et al., 
2007). Second equilibration was performed at NPT ensemble for 1 ns, with timestep of 0.001ps. Constant 
temperature was maintained at 310K using v-rescale thermostat, and constant pressure was maintained 
at 1 bar using Parinello-Rahman barostat (Parrinello & Rahman, 1981). Following the equilibrations, the 
position restraints were removed and production simulations were performed for 600 ns with 0.002ps 
timestep. The above-mentioned protocol was repeated in 3 more replicates, with resulting total sam­
pling of 2.4ls in four replicates. The same methodology was also repeated for O-GlcNAcylated, 
Phosphorylated and unmodified Akt2 systems. Further details of the modeled simulation systems and 
simulations are summarized in supplementary Table 1.
Similarly, another system consisting of O-GlcNAcylated and Phosphorylated Akt2 was modeled, where 
the endogenous substrates (PIP3, ATP and Mg2þ) were bound to the respective cavities. The endogen­
ous substrates were docked using GPU-accelerated version of AutoDock4 (Santos-Martins et al., 2021), 
where the docking grid of 51x51x51 Å was set around the respective binding cavities, and spacing of 
0.375 Å were set. A total of 2,000 docked confromations were generated for each ligand, and the com­
plex with least predicted binding free energy was chosen for further simulations. The post-translations 
on the docked structures were modeled using the same protocol and force fields as mentioned above, 
and the modeled structures were subject to preparatory MD steps using the same protocol and parame­
ters as mentioned above. The force fields for the endogenous substrate molecules were described 
using the CGenFF (Vanommeslaeghe et al., 2010; Vanommeslaeghe & MacKerell, 2012). The bound 
O-GlcNAcylated and Phosphorylated Akt2 systems were then subjected to 200 ns production simulations 
using GROMACS-2020 (Kutzner et al., 2019). The details of the modeled simulation systems and trajecto­
ries are provided in supplementary Table 1.
Trajectory analysis: secondary structure analysis
Following MD simulations, all the replicate trajectories from each PTM state were concatenated into a tra­
jectory ensemble with 2.4ls sampling each. All the analyses were performed on this trajectory ensemble. 
Secondary structure analysis was performed using the gmx do_dssp tool of GROMACS, using DSSP-3.0.0 
(Kabsch & Sander, 1983; Kutzner et al., 2019). From this calculation, time-resolved DSSP maps of O- 
GlcNAcylated versus Phosphorylated Akt2 were plotted and visualized.
Residue fluctuation calculations
All the following analysis steps were performed using GROMACS-2020(Berendsen et al., 1984; Kutzner 
et al., 2019), and all the protein structure visualizations were performed using ChimeraX (Meng et al., 
2023), unless mentioned otherwise. To identify the residue fluctuations associated with each of the 
modified states, as well as in unmodified Akt2, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) calculations were 
performed. RMSF calculations were performed on the C-alpha atoms of the Akt2 residues for all the 
three states. The functional domains and the functionally significant residues were mapped on the RMSF 
plot. This information was retrieved from the InterPro server, based on the annotations from the con­
served domain database (Paysan-Lafosse et al., 2023; J. Wang et al., 2023).
Solvent accessibility calculations
To understand the changes in the solvent accessibility of the functionally important active site, PIP3 
binding site and activation loop residues of Akt2, absolute solvent accessible surface area was calculated 
using gmx sasa (Eisenhaber et al., 1995). For the calculations, the probe size of 0.14 nm was used, and 
the average of the absolute solvent accessible surface area, over all the simulation frames with the 
standard deviation was calculated for both O-GlcNAcylated and Phosphorylated states. Based on the 
average absolute solvent accessibility values, relative solvent accessibility of the given residue was calcu­
lated according to the equation (1):

RMSD-based clustering and visualization
To identify the most representative conformations associated with the O-GlcNAcylated and phosphorylated 
states of Akt2, RMSD-based clustering calculations were performed on both the trajectory ensembles. 
Gromos method was used for clustering of conformations (Daura et al., 1999). For the O-GlcNAcylated 
Akt2 trajectory, the RMSD cutoff of 0.52 nm was used. For the phosphorylated Akt2 trajectory, the RMSD 
cutoff of 0.48 nm was used. The cutoff values were selected in such a way that the top-5 clusters account 
for at least 90% of all the trajectory frames. The cluster with the highest population was visualized, and 
population of the top cluster was noted.
Cavity calculation
To identify the cavities associated with the O-GlcNAcylated and phosphorylated Akt2 states, the cavities 
were detected from the respective representative snapshots using pyKVFinder module of ChimeraX 
(Guerra et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2023). For the cavity detection, the grid size of 0.6 Å, inner probe of 
1.4 Å radius and outer probe of 4.0 Å radius was employed. Once the cavities were detected, their vol­
umes were calculated and the cavities were visualized. The cavities adjacent to the Akt2 active site and 
the cavity separating Kinase and Pleckstrein Homology (PH) domains were identified and visualized.
Tunnel identification
To evaluate if a pathway exists for entry of substrates around the Akt1 activation loop, tunnels were 
mapped onto the O-GlcNAcylated and Phosphorylated Akt2 trajectory snapshots. An ensemble consist­
ing of snapshots from second half of a trajectory replicate (300, 400 and 500 ns) was created for each of 
O-GlcNAcylated and Phosphorylated replicates. Tunnel mapping was done on these ensembles using 
CAVER-analyst program (Jurcik et al., 2018). For this calculation, the residues of activation loop (residues 
310-317) were selected to map the tunnels. The probe radius of 0.9 Å was chosen, shell depth of 4 Å 
and shell radius of 3 Å were set for the calculation. Clustering threshold was set to 3.5 Å. From the 
detected tunnels, the average bottleneck radius and the throughput scores were noted and compared.
Protein structure network and hydrogen bond analysis
To identify the differences with the protein structure network associated with O-GlcNAcylated versus 
phosphorylated Akt2 states, protein structure network of both the states were mapped. The protein 
structure network was constructed from the respective trajectories using MD-TASK (Brown et al., 2017). 
From the calculated network, average betweenness centrality over all the trajectory frames was calcu­
lated for all the residues. The betweenness centrality profile was plotted for both the O-GlcNAcylated 
and phosphorylated trajectories and compared. Hydrogen bonds were calculated in two ways: On the 
representative structures with each PTM obtained using the methodology described above, the hydro­
gen bonds were identified using ChimeraX (Meng et al., 2023). The distance cutoff was used for various 
donor-acceptor groups based on previous analysis of hydrogen bonded distances from experimentally 
solved structures (Mills & Dean, 1996). In addition, the frequency of all the hydrogen bonded contacts in 
respective trajectories were calculated using MDAnalysis and VMD program (Humphrey et al., 1996; 
Michaud-Agrawal et al., 2011). In the O-GlcNAcylated trajectory, contact frequency was calculated

Evaluation of Akt2 autoinhibition
To check if the Akt2 trajectories in absence of substrates sampled the autoinhibited state, the phosphory­
lated and O-GlcNAcylated trajectories with endogenous substrates were subjected to centre of mass (CoM) 
distance calculations. The centre of mass of both PH domains and Kinase domains of Akt2 were defined 
based on the domain boundary information from conserved domain database (J. Wang et al., 2023). The 
distance between centre of mass of these domains were calculated using gmx distance tool of GROMACS, 
and the CoM distance as a function of time was plotted and visualized. The same calculation was also per­
formed for the first 200 ns of all the phosphorylated Akt2 replicates without endogenous substrates, as 
well as on the first replicate trajectories of O-GlcNAcylated Akt2. Similarly, the conformations of X-ray crys­
tallographic structure of autoinhibited Akt1 (PDB: 4EJN)(Ashwell et al., 2012) were compared with the last 
frames from all the phosphorylated trajectories using ChimeraX (Meng et al., 2023). For least square fitting, 
long atom pairs were pruned, using a cutoff distance of 3 Å, and C-alpha RMSD were calculated on the 
superimposed structures. Radius of gyration of the Akt2 was calculated for all the phosphorylated trajecto­
ries and plotted both as absolute values and probability distribution, using gmx gyrate tool of GROMACS.
Results and discussion
O-GlcNAc modification alters Akt2 secondary structure at a local level
Earlier experimental studies have suggested that O-GlcNAc and phosphorylation modifications modulate 
the protein secondary structure at a local level. To investigate if such changes occur in the 
O-GlcNAcylated and phosphorylated Akt2, we calculated the most prevalent secondary structure of Akt2 
residues in all the MD simulations, through time-resolved DSSP maps of Akt2 in O-GlcNAcylated versus 
phosphorylated trajectories While the overall secondary structure patterns are unchanged between both 
the forms of Akt2, we note few variations in the secondary structure at the local level: In the 
O-GlcNAcylated state, the residues 55-65 present adjacent to the PIP3 binding cavity is present as a 
b-sheet. However, this secondary structure is lost in the phosphorylated state, and these residues are 
instead present in the coil form (Figure 2). Similarly, in the activation loop, this region is present in the 
coil conformation in the O-GlcNAcylated state, whereas this region is present in the bend or turn con­
formation in the phosphorylated state. The coil conformation in the O-GlcNAcylated state suggests that 
presence of O-GlcNAc in this region may introduce restrictions on the conformational flexibility of this 
region, forcing it to adopt a linear coil conformation. On the other hand, phosphorylation in this region 
may further provide flexibility to adopt diverse conformations, which further may aid this region to 
accommodate the substrate peptides due to flexibility in conformational exploration. This observation is 
also consistent with the observations from the RMSF calculations, where activation loop is more flexible 
in the Phosphorylated Akt2.
Activation loop of Akt2 is more dynamic in the phosphorylated state
Since earlier studies observed conformational changes at the local level in O-GlcNAcylated and phos­
phorylated states (Chen et al., 2006; Elbaum & Zondlo, 2014; Rani & Mallajosyula, 2021; S. Wang et al., 
2012), we investigated if similar changes can be observed in the Akt2. In this direction, we calculated 
Root Mean Square Fluctuations (RMSF) on the Akt2 C-alpha atoms in O-GlcNAcylated, phosphorylated 
and unmodified states. To understand if such changes are occurring at any of the functionally significant 
residues, we mapped such residues and examined the RMSF of these residues in each of the states. 
From this comparison, we observed that globally, presence of PTM has a significant stabilizing effect

phosphorylation modifications were found to have a stabilizing effect as compared to the peptide with 
no modifications (Chen et al., 2006). However, on comparison of RMSF between O-GlcNAcylated versus 
phosphorylated states, more substantial differences at functionally important residues can be observed. 
The activation loop, whose dynamics is required for stable binding of peptide substrate to the Akt2 
active site, is highly flexible in the phosphorylated state, with RMSF of upto 0.6 nm in phosphorylated 
state (Figure 3). This region is more rigid in the O-GlcNAcylated state, with RMSF ranging between 0.3- 
0.4 nm. Since the activation loop has significant involvement in facilitating the substrate binding and sta­
bilization of the substrate on the active site, the increased flexibility associated with phosphorylated 
state could play a role in facilitating substrate binding. On the other hand, the rigidity of activation loop 
in the O-GlcNAcylated state may impede the substrate binding to the active site. To ensure that these 
observations hold true at the level of individual replicates as well, we performed the same calculations

O-GlcNAcylation results in burial of key active site and activation loop residues
In addition to the dynamics, we also hypothesized that the crosstalk between both the modifications 
may also affect the accessibility of the critical functional residues of Akt2, including the active site. To 
test this, we calculated the average of absolute solvent accessible surface area of the active site and acti­
vation loop residues of Akt2 in O-GlcNAcylated versus phosphorylated states. Based on this, we further 
calculated the relative solvent accessibility, and classified the residues as accessible or buried, based on 
the RSA cutoff of 15. From these calculations, we made the following observations: Remarkably, we 
found that the RSA of most of the functional site residues were higher in the phosphorylated state than 
in the O-GlcNAcylated state (Table 1). In addition, most of the activation loop residues are buried in O- 
GlcNAcylated state, in contrast to phosphorylated state, where the residues are accessible. Most of the 
active site residues do not undergo changes in their accessibility states in O-GlcNAcylated and phos­
phorylated states. But interestingly, some positively charged residues in the active site that are buried in 
the O-GlcNAcylated state are accessible in the phosphorylated state. These include the residue K181 and 
the residue K277, which are critical for ATP binding and catalytic activity of Akt2 (Table 1). K181 particu­
larly is a critical active site residue, as it stabilizes ATP binding through formation of salt bridges 
between the phospate moieties of ATP (Vivanco et al., 2014). Similarly, in Akt1, the residue K277 is an 
important regulator of Akt activity, where its SUMOylation results in increased Akt activity without affect­
ing its phosphorylation (Lin et al., 2016). Additionally, we also observed increased accessibility of the 
proton acceptor residue D275 in phosphorylated state (14.4) as compared to the O-GlcNAcylated state 
(9.7, Table 1). Though the increased accessibility does not translate to accessible state of D275, the resi­
due may be accessible for significant portion of the trajectory, considering the standard deviation associ­
ated with the accessibility value. Accessibility calculations show the atomic basis of Akt2 inactivation 
due to O-GlcNAcylation. O-GlcNAcylation may result in blocking the access of these residues to exogen­
ous molecules, possibly by the N-acetylglucosamine. This further may result in burial of the key active 
site and activation loop residues, as observed in our simulations (Table 1). We also performed this ana­
lysis on the level of individual replicates. From this, we found that that much of activation loop is buried 
in all the O-GlcNAcylated replicates compared to phosphorylated replicates, as observed from low mar­
gin between upper and lower bounds at 95% confidence interval (Supplementary table 3).
O-GlcNAcylated and phosphorylated Akt2 sampled different conformations with substantial 
changes in the active site cavity
Since we observed that some of the critical active site and activation loop residues are buried in 
O-GlcNAcylated states, we further hypothesized that this may be accompanied with significant shifts in 
the Akt2 as well as PTM conformations in respective simulation trajectories. To get a better understand­
ing of the changes occurring at the structural level, we performed RMSD-based clustering, where we 
extracted representative snapshots with highest population and visualized the same. These snapshots 
represent 24.6% and 25.4% of trajectories in the O-GlcNAcylated state and phosphorylated states 
respectively. This visualization provided more clear differences on the structural shifts in Akt2 associated 
with both the PTMs. In the O-GlcNAcylated state, it is evident that the access to the activation loop is 
blocked due to the presence of O-GlcNAc residue, which appears to act as a ‘gate’ preventing the entry

Experimental studies on Akt1 have shown that in presence of O-GlcNAc modification, phosphorylation 
of Akt1 is impaired (S. Wang et al., 2012b). The same observations were also made on Akt2 in context of 
insulin signalling (Soesanto et al., 2008b). Phosphorylation of T309 residue of Akt2 is essential for its 
functional activation rather than determining substrate specificity, as absence of phosphorylation on the 
residue T308 of Akt1 renders Akt1 completely inactive (N. Kumar et al., 2007). The phosphorylation at 
the T309 residue is required for stable ATP binding, as in the unphosphorylated state, ATP binding 
remains unstable due to steric hindrance caused by lack of N. Kumar et al., (2007). Thus, we propose the 
O-GlcNAc-Phosphorylation crosstalk as an on-off regulatory mechanism that determines Akt activity.
To further support this observation, we hypothesized that this may be accompanied with changes in 
the cavity size. To test this, we detected the cavities and calculated cavity volumes of both the represen­
tative structures. But surprisingly, we found that the conformation in the O-GlcNAcylated state has larger 
cavity associated with the active site than that of phosphorylated state (Figure 4C,D). Similarly, the 
O-GlcNAcylated conformation has a larger cavity between the kinase and the Pleckstrein Homology (PH) 
domains. So how did the O-GlcNAcylation result in lower accessibility of the active site despite larger 
cavity volume associated with the active site and larger cavity separating the PH and kinase domains?

To address this question, we examined protein structural network associated with the Akt2 trajectories 
in O-GlcNAcylated state and phosphorylated state, based on the dynamics of backbone C-alpha atoms.
Phosphorylated Akt2 is consists of biologically relevant tunnels that facilitate entry of substrate 
into the activation loop
Since we previously showed that O-GlcNAcylation resulted in burial of the Akt2 activation loop, we fur­
ther examined if there is a presence of tunnel around this region in O-GlcNAcylated versus 
Phosphorylated states. For this, we calculated the presence of tunnels in last 50% of trajectory snapshots 
(300, 400 and 500 ns) from each replicates of O-GlcNAcylated and Phosphorylated Akt2 trajectories. 
From this calculation, we evaluated average bottleneck radius and throughput value. Bottleneck radius 
provides an indication of whether substrates can pass through the tunnel, and throughput provides an 
indication of whether the tunnel is capable of supporting transport of substrate molecules. In CAVER, a 
tunnel throughput value of >0.8 signifies the tunnel to be biologically relevant. In good agreement with 
the solvent accessibility calculations, 3 out of 4 phosphorylated replicates were found to have tunnels 
with high throughput scores (>0.8, Table 2), with the exception of 4th replicate. In contrast to this, the 
activation loop in O-GlcNAc state was found to have low throughput tunnels in 3 out of 4 replicates, 
apart from the exception of 3rd replicate. Similarly, the tunnels in O-GlcNAcylated Akt2 were also charac­
terized by substantially smaller bottleneck radii (Table 2). In the Phosphorylated state, all the tunnels 
have bottleneck radii >1.5 Å, while in the O-GlcNAcylated state, 3 of the 4 replicates have bottleneck 
radii <1Å, indicating that the tunnels in O-GlcNAcylated state are not conducive for the entry of sub­
strates into the activation loop.
O-GlcNAcylated and phosphorylated Akt2 are characterized by a network of hydrogen-bonded 
contacts
From the protein structural network, we calculated betweenness centrality of the residues in the 
O-GlcNAcylated and phosphorylated trajectories, which hints towards the extent of inter-residue contacts 
formed by the residue in the trajectories. We observed that in the O-GlcNAcylated state, two groups of 
residues, one around the residue G312 in the activation loop and other being the residues K14 and G16 
at the PIP3 binding site have high betweenness centrality, compared to phosphorylated state (Figure 5).
Since the residue G312 is adjacent to the O-GlcNAcylated T313, we also checked if the carbohydrates 
in the O-GlcNAcylated state themselves are involved in hydrogen bonds with Akt2 residues, since earlier 
studies on the conformational effects of O-GlcNAc modification have suggested that the O-GlcNAc forms 
localized hydrogen bonds with adjacent residues (Toleman et al., 2018). Interestingly, we found that the 
O-GlcNAc connected to the residue T313 formed hydrogen bond with the residue T82 in the representa­
tive structure (Figure 6A). Since this contact involves interaction between the O-GlcNAc of the kinase 
domain and the T82 residue of the PH domain, this hydrogen bond may be the cause of the low acces­
sibility of the active site and activation loop in the O-GlcNAcylated state. To further confirm if this con­
formational state persists in the entire O-GlcNAcylated trajectory, we performed contact frequency 
analysis, where the residues forming contacts (interaction distance <0.5 nm) with the O-GlcNAc on T313 
were characterized and the frequency of contacts were calculated.

Phosphorylated Akt2 is characterized by contacts between the phosphorylated threonine and 
PIP3-binding residues
Apart from the O-GlcNAcylated trajectory, we also examined if similar hydrogen bonded networks also 
exist in the phosphorylated state, based on observations from the representative phosphorylated con­
formation as well as from the protein structural network. We also observed that the phosphorylated tra­
jectory have certain functionally significant residues having high betweenness centrality. For instance, 
the active site residues T213 and E230 were found to have high betweenness centrality (Figure 5). This 
can be attributed to the presence of hydrogen bond between both the residues in the representative 
phsphorylated conformation (Figure 6C). Similarly, we also checked if the phosphorylated residues form 
any hydrogen bonded contacts in the representative structure. In good agreement with the previous 
studies on Akt1 structure, we found that the ATP-binding R274 residue formed high-frequency hydrogen 
bonded contacts (12.5% of the trajectory) with the phosphorylated T309 (Lu et al., 2015; Siess & 
Leonard, 2019)(Supplementary table 4). But interestingly, we also found that the phosphorylated T309 
formed similar contacts with another residue, the K14 located in PH domain (Figure 6D). These pair of 
residues are seen to form hydrogen bonds for 8.9% of the trajectory (Supplementary table 4). We further 
propose the representative phosphorylated conformations observed in our simulations are stabilized by 
these contacts, which involve hydrogen bonds. Apart from K14, the phosphorylated T309 is also 
observed to form contacts with the residues E17 and R23, accounting for 7.9% of simulation trajectory 
each (Supplementary table 4). Remarkably, all these residues are charged in nature, and all three of 
them are involved in interaction with PIP3 (Miao et al., 2010). Since the PIP3 interacting residues form 
the hydrogen-bonded contacts with the phosphorylated T309, the conformation of these residues 
sampled in the simulations may not favor binding of PIP3. Earlier studies have suggested that despite 
phosphorylation, protein kinase-B autoinhibits itself in absence of PIP3 binding (Truebestein et al., 2021). 
The separation of PH domain from the kinase domain was found to occur only after PIP3 binding and 
subsequent attachment of PH domain to the membrane. Thus, we propose that this conformation is 
associated with the autoinhibited phosphorylated state of Akt2, through formation of hydrogen bonded 
contacts between phosphorylated T309 and PIP3 binding residues in the PH domain. These contacts fur­
ther augment the hydrogen bonded contacts between the R274 and phosphorylated T309 which has 
been characterized earlier from studies on Akt1 (Lu et al., 2015; Siess & Leonard, 2019). Due to low cavity 
volume of the active site (Figure 4D), the phosphatase binding to this region remains unfavorable. 
Through this mechanism, the T309-PIP3 binding site interaction provides shielding against dephosphory­
lation of Akt2 by phosphatases.
Comparison of Akt2 dynamics in substrate-free versus substrate-bound states show that PIP3 
binding results in partial activation of Akt2
We further modeled Akt2 system with ATP, Mg2þ and PIP3 headgroups bound to respective cavities, in 
both O-GlcNAcylated as well as in phosphorylated states, and performed short simulations (200 ns each). 
To evaluate if Akt conformation in phosphorylated state corresponds to autoinhibition, we measured 
centre of mass (CoM) distance between PH domains and Kinase domains among phosphorylated Akt2 
w/ATP,Mg2þ, PIP3, O-GlcNAcylated Akt2 w/ATP,Mg2þ,PIP3, the first 200 ns of phosphorylated Akt2 repli­
cates without substrates and the first 200 ns of O-GlcNAcylated Akt2 replicate-1 without substrates. We 
performed this analysis due to the fact that Akt activation is characterized by separation of PH domain 
and Kinase domains. Interestingly, the phosphorylated Akt2 in absence of PIP3 has lesser CoM distance 
between both the domains, compared to the phosphorylated Akt2 in presence of substrates (Figure 7). 
Difference in CoM distance of as much as 0.5 nm (5 Å) was found, which is higher than the difference in 
CoM distance between O-GlcNAcylated Akt2 and Phosphorylated Akt2 with substrates. Furthermore, in 
the O-GlcNAcylated state, the PIP3 binding did not necessarily lead to the separation of PH domain and

Kinase domain, suggesting that despite PIP3 binding, O-GlcNAcylated Akt2 remains in inactive state. 
These observations indicate that in phosphorylated Akt2, PIP3 binding triggers the separation of PH and 
Kinase domains, as seen from increased CoM distance between both the domains. In absence of PIP3, 
both the domains maintain tight contact, as observed from the contacts between phosphorylated threo­
nine and the PIP3-binding K14 residue (Figure 6D), and from the lower CoM distance between both the 
domains (Figure 7). Though the CoM distance increased in our simulations of PIP3-bound Akt2, we 
emphasize that only the partially activated form of Akt2 would have been sampled in the simulations 
with PIP3. The full activation will require binding of the PH domain to the membrane, upon which the 
CoM distance between both the domains will further increase. Thus, we are able to demonstrate here 
that unless PIP3 binds to the phosphorylated Akt2, the phosphorylated Akt2 will remain in an autoinhib­
ited state.
Apart from the CoM distance, we also calculated the C-alpha RMSD between the final frame snap­
shots of each phosphorylated replicate and the X-ray crystallographic structure of autoinhibited Akt1 
(PDB: 4EJN). Except replicate-4, all the other replicates have RMSD between 6-7Å with respect to the 
autoinhibited Akt1 structure. On the other hand, PIP3 bound partially activated phosphorylated Akt2 
conformation was found to have a higher RMSD of 7.9 Å (Supplementary table 5). Though the RMSD is 
still at a higher level between the phosphorylated Akt2 and autoinhibited Akt1, the higher RMSD could 
be attributed to the differences between Akt1 and Akt2 at the level of sequences.
Additionally, since our simulations also include the presence of ATP and Mg2þ in the active site cav­
ity, we evaluated if the presence of these ligands increased the accessibility of activation loop residues, 
which were buried in O-GlcNAcylated Akt2 in absence of substrates (Table 1). Despite the presence of 
ATP and Mg2þ, the activation loop residues are equally inaccessible in O-GlcNACylated state (RSA < 15) 
compared to the phosphorylated state, despite the presence of PIP3 and ATP & Mg2þ bound to the 
Akt2 as substrates (Supplementary table 6). These observations further strengthen our argument that 
O-GlcNAcylation further hinders the accessibility of functionally important residues.
Our initial hypothesis was that the O-GlcNAc modification would also restrict PIP3 binding site acces­
sibility apart from reducing the accessibility of the active site of the Kinase domain. However, the obser­
vations from the protein structure network calculations (Figure 5) and visualization of the representative 
conformations (Figure 6) suggested the contrary. Surprisingly, the calculations of solvent accessibility of 
PIP3 binding site show that this site is more accessible in the O-GlcNAcylated state rather than in the 
phosphorylated state, particularly for the positively charged residues such as K14 and R25 (Table 1). To 
check if such conformational changes are localized or if they are global in nature, we calculated the 
radius of gyration and compared its distribution between the O-GlcNAcylated and Phosphorylated forms 
with as well as without PIP3 binding. These calculations further indicated that these changes are local­
ized in nature, as seen from smaller difference in radius of gyration distribution between the Akt2 forms 
lacking PIP3 binding, as opposed to the radius of gyration distribution of Phosphorylated Akt2 bound to 
PIP3 and ATP (Supplementary figure 3B).

Lack of a lipid bilayer in the simulation that simulates the PIP3 binding and the complete activation 
of Akt2 is indeed a limitation of this study. But even in the absence of a lipid bilayer and a rather sim­
plistic representation of PIP3 in this work in form of a head group, we still observed the separation of 
kinase and PH domains in our simulations, evident from higher centre of mass distance between both 
the domains as compared to phosphorylated state in absence of PIP3 headgroups (Figure 7). 
Additionally, we also calculated radius of gyration (Rg) of phosphorylated replicates in absence of PIP3 
and compared with the simulation with PIP3. We compared the Rg data from our simulations with the 
Rg data from similar SAXS experiments on Akt1 (Truebestein et al., 2021b). Though the Rg values 
observed in SAXS experiments could not be entirely replicated in this work, we found broad agreement 
with the trends in the Rg distribution compared with the experiments. SAXS experiments showed that 
Akt1 has considerably higher Rg upon PIP3 binding (3.1 nm) as opposed to the absence of PIP3 (2.7 nm). 
Consistent with this, we also found higher Rg of Akt2 in presence of PIP3 binding as opposed to phos­
phorylated Akt2 in absence of PIP3 (Supplementary figure 3A and B). With these observations, we infer 
that despite the limitations of our simulations, we were able to capture the conformational transition 
associated with PIP3 binding to a certain extent.
Conclusion
We describe at the atomic level how the opposing PTMs O-GlcNAcylation and Phosphorylation form a 
cross-talk mechanism in Akt2 through modulating the dynamics and solvent accessibility of key Akt2 res­
idues. The lack of accessibility of these residues in O-GlcNAcylated state implies that Akt2 remains 
inactive in the O-GlcNAcylated state. On the other hand, though these residues are solvent accessible in 
the phosphorylated state, they form an elaborate interaction network involving hydrogen bonds and 
salt bridges, that shield the phosphorylated T309 residue from degradation by phosphatases and keep 
the Akt2 in autoinhibited state. Having said that, some aspects of this problem were not addressed by 
this work, which may form the basis for future studies: The findings from this work were not validated 
through experimental approaches. This may be done in future through solid state, solution NMR or 
X-ray crystallographic studies. The findings from this work also open up the possibility of discovery or 
development of therapeutic agents for type-II diabetes, that may either prevent O-GlcNAcylation of Akt2 
by O-GlcNAc transferases, or that may rescue the Akt2 activity through binding to an allosteric site of 
Akt2. Such agents may be discovered or designed through in silico approaches, and their biological 
activity can be validated through cell-free assays, in vitro and in vivo methods.
