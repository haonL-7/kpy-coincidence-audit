A B S T R A C T  
Protein lysine modifications (PLMs) are hotspots of post-translational modifications and are involved in many 
diseases; however, their role in human sperm remains obscure. This study examined the presence and functional 
roles of a classical PLM (lysine acetylation, Kac) and three novel PLMs (lysine malonylation, Kmal; lysine suc­
cinylation, Ksucc; lysine crotonylation, Kcr) in human sperm. Immunoblotting and immunofluorescence assays 
revealed modified proteins (15–150 kDa) in the tails of human sperm. An immunoaffinity approach coupled with 
liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry revealed 1423 Kac sites in 680 proteins, 196 Kmal sites in 
118 proteins, 788 Ksucc sites in 251 proteins, and 1836 Kcr sites in 645 proteins. These modified proteins 
participate in a variety of biological processes and metabolic pathways. Crosstalk analysis demonstrated that 
proteins involved in the sperm energy pathways of glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, the citrate cycle, fatty 
acid oxidation, and ketone body metabolism were modified by at least one of these modifications. In addition, 
these modifications were found in 62 male fertility-related proteins that weave a protein-protein interaction 
network associated with asthenoteratozoospermia, asthenozoospermia, globozoospermia, spermatogenic failure, 
hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and polycystic kidney disease. Our findings shed light on the functional role of 
PLMs in male reproduction. 
Significance: Protein lysine modifications (PLMs) are hotspots of posttranslational modifications and are involved 
in many diseases. This study revealed the presence of a classical PLM (lysine acetylation) and three novel PLMs 
(lysine malonylation, lysine succinylation, and lysine crotonylation) in human sperm tails. The modified proteins 
participate in a variety of biological processes and metabolic pathways. In addition, these modifications were 
found in 62 male infertility-associated proteins and could serve as potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic 
targets for male infertility.   
1. Introduction 
Infertility is a major health challenge globally, and one in six adults 
currently suffer from infertility [1]. In China, one-quarter of couples 
under reproductive age suffer infertility [2]. Studies indicate that male 
factors account for approximately 50% of infertility cases [3]. The ul­
timate executor of male reproduction is sperm, which fertilizes the egg, 
thus initiating the life cycle. The quantity and quality of sperm are 
essential for male fertility and are under complicated and precise 
regulation. Abnormalities in these processes may cause male infertility 
[4]. Therefore, it is meaningful to study the molecular mechanisms of 
spermatogenesis and sperm function to uncover the pathogenesis of 
male infertility and develop possible treatments.

that diversify protein functions, regulate cellular processes, and 
dynamically coordinate signaling networks [5]. Because proteins cannot 
be synthesized in sperm, PTMs of existing proteins are the key mecha­
nisms involved in regulating protein function [6]. A well-studied PTM in 
mammalian sperm is phosphorylation. The signaling cascade induced by 
phosphorylation is an important means to regulate spermatogenesis, 
sperm motility, sperm capacitation, sperm hyperactivation, and the 
acrosome reaction of sperm [7–11]. Although lysine is a hot topic of 
PTMs and protein lysine modifications (PLMs) are important PTMs that 
regulate cell function, the roles of PLMs in sperm are poorly known. Four 
classical PLMs, lysine methylation, acetylation (Kac), ubiquitylation, 
and SUMOylation, have been identified in human sperm and are 
involved in the regulation of spermatogenesis and sperm function 
[5,6,12–15]. In recent years, dozens of novel PLMs have been identified, 
such as 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib), glutarylation (Kglu), succiny­
lation (Ksucc), crotonylation (Kcr), and malonylation (Kmal) [16,17]. In 
our recent study, we found that Khib and Kglu are present in human 
sperm and play roles in the regulation of human sperm motility [18,19]. 
In addition, higher levels of Khib and lower levels of Kglu were detected 
in the sperm of asthenozoospermic men than in those of normozoo­
spermic men [18,19]. These results suggest that PLMs are important for 
the regulation of sperm functions. 
Compared with Kac, which adds two carbons (–COCH3) to lysine, 
Kmal, Ksucc, and Kcr introduce bulkier acyl groups to lysine: three 
(–COCH2COO−), 
four 
(–COCH2CH2COO−), 
and 
four 
carbons 
(–COCHCHCH3), respectively [16,17]. In addition, Kmal and Ksucc are 
acidic PLMs that introduce acidic carboxylic groups and change the 
charge on lysine from +1 to −1 under physiological pH [16,17]. These 
changes in protein charge are similar to those in protein phosphoryla­
tion. It is conceivable that Kmal, Ksucc, and Kcr have more profound 
impacts on protein structure and function than Kac. The global malo­
nylome, succinylome, and crotonylome of human somatic cells revealed 
that these PLMs are important for regulating cellular metabolism in 
pathophysiological states [20–22]. These modified proteins are involved 
in energy metabolism, including fatty acid metabolism, oxidative 
phosphorylation, glycolysis, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle [20–22]. 
Since these energy metabolism pathways are also important for sperm 
functions, Kmal, Ksucc, and Kcr may participate in the regulation of 
sperm function [23]. However, global proteomic analyses of lysine 
acetylation, malonylation, succinylation, and crotonylation have not 
been performed in human sperm. In this study, we used an immu­
noaffinity approach coupled with liquid chromatography/tandem mass 
spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to globally characterize acetylation, succi­
nylation, crotonylation, and malonylation, as well as their crosstalk, in 
human sperm. These results may provide new insight into the functional 
roles of novel PLMs in human sperm. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Main reagents 
Anti-acetyllysine mouse mAb (PTM-101), anti-acetyllysine antibody- 
conjugated agarose beads (PTM-104), anti-succinyllysine mouse mAb 
(PTM-419), anti-succinyllysine antibody-conjugated agarose beads 
(PTM-402), anti-malonyllysine rabbit pAb (PTM-901), and anti- 
malonyllysine antibody-conjugated agarose beads (PTM-904), anti- 
crotonyllysine rabbit pAb (PTM-501), and anti-crotonyllysine anti­
body-conjugated agarose beads (PTM-503) were purchased from PTM 
BioLabs Inc. (Hangzhou, China). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated 
goat anti-rabbit, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse, 
DyLight 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (or mouse), and DyLight 488- 
conjugated goat anti-mouse antibodies were purchased from Thermo 
Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA). The 4% paraformaldehyde, 
phosphate-buffered saline, Triton X-100, and 10% normal goat serum 
were purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, 
China). The 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole, sodium dodecyl sulfate, 
dithiothreitol, trypsin, iodoacetamide, trifluoroacetic acid, formic acid, 
and acetonitrile were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. 
Louis, MO, USA). The protease inhibitor cocktail, trichostatin A, and 
nicotinamide were purchased from MedchemExpress LLC. (Monmouth 
Junction, NJ, USA). 
2.2. Semen sample collection and treatment 
After 3–5 days of sexual abstinence, semen samples were obtained 
from young volunteers who visited the reproductive medical center at 
Jiangxi Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Nanchang, China) via 
masturbation. Ten normozoospermic men were recruited for this study. 
The fertile men with normal semen quality were classified as normo­
zoospermic men according to the World Health Organization laboratory 
manual for the examination and processing of human semen (fifth edi­
tion, http://www.who.int/reproductivehealth/publications/infertility/ 
9789241547789/en/). The semen parameters are shown in Table S1. 
2.3. Protein preparation 
Sperm from each normozoospermic man were first treated with so­
matic cell lysis buffer (0.2% sodium dodecyl sulfate and 0.5% Triton X- 
100 in phosphate-buffered saline) to eliminate residual somatic cells. 
After treatment, the sperm were sonicated on ice using a high-intensity 
ultrasonic processor (Scientz, Ningbo, China) in 4 volumes of lysis buffer 
(8 mol/L urea, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 3 μM trichostatin A, 50 
mM nicotinamide, and 2 mM EDTA). The remaining debris was removed 
by centrifugation at 12,000g at 4 ◦C for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant 
was collected and the protein concentration was determined with a 
bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) according to 
the manufacturer’s instructions. In total, sperm proteins were isolated 
from 10 normozoospermic men and subjected to immunoblotting assay, 
trypsin digestion, and immunoaffinity enrichment. 
2.4. Immunoblotting assay 
Thirty micrograms of protein were taken from each normozoosper­
mic protein sample and pooled together to obtain a protein pool. Sub­
sequently, 20 μg of protein from the protein pool was used for 
immunoblotting assays as previously described [18,19]. The dilution 
was 1:1000 for the anti-acetyllysine mouse mAb, the anti-succinyllysine 
mouse mAb, the anti-malonyllysine rabbit pAb, and the anti- 
crotonyllysine rabbit pAb. The dilutions were 1:10000 for the horse­
radish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (or mouse) antibody. 
2.5. Immunofluorescence assay 
Immunofluorescence staining was conducted according to our pre­
viously published methods [18,19]. The sperm samples were fixed with 
4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline, permeabilized with 
0.2% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline and blocked with 10% 
normal goat serum in phosphate-buffered saline. The sperm were then 
incubated with rabbit IgG (negative control), an anti-acetyllysine mouse 
mAb, an anti-succinyllysine mouse mAb, an anti-malonyllysine rabbit 
pAb, or an anti-crotonyllysine rabbit pAb at a dilution of 1:100 over­
night. Subsequently, the sperm were stained with DyLight 488-conju­
gated goat anti-rabbit (or mouse) secondary antibody (1:200 dilution) 
and 0.5 μM 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 1 h. The stained sperm 
were imaged using a Nikon A1 confocal laser microscope (Nikon Co. 
Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). 
2.6. Trypsin digestion and immunoaffinity enrichment 
Four milligrams of protein were taken from each normozoospermic 
protein sample, pooled together, and evenly divided into 4 groups, each 
containing 10 mg of protein. The protein in each group was reduced

with 5 mM dithiothreitol for 30 min at 56 ◦C and alkylated with 11 mM 
iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in darkness. The treated 
protein was then diluted by adding 100 mM NH4HCO3 to a urea con­
centration <2 mol/L. Finally, the protein was digested by trypsin at 1:50 
trypsin-to-protein (w/w) for the first digestion overnight and at 1:100 
trypsin-to-protein (w/w) for a second 4-h digestion. 
The tryptic peptides were fractionated by high pH reverse-phase 
HPLC. A thermo Betasil C18 column (5 μm particles, 10 mm ID, 250 
mm length) was used. Briefly, peptides were first separated with a 
gradient of 8% to 32% acetonitrile (pH 9.0) over 60 min into 60 frac­
tions. Then, the peptides were combined into fractions and dried by 
vacuum centrifugation. 
To enrich the modified peptides, 4 mg of digested peptide was dis­
solved in immunoprecipitation buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 
mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0). The modified peptides were 
immunoprecipitated by 80 μL of anti-acetyllysine antibody-conjugated 
agarose beads, anti-succinyllysine antibody-conjugated agarose beads, 
anti-crotonyllysine antibody-conjugated agarose beads, and anti- 
malonyllysine antibody-conjugated agarose beads. The bound peptides 
were eluted from the beads with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The eluted 
fractions were combined and vacuum-dried. For LC-MS/MS analysis, the 
resulting peptides were desalted with C18 ZipTips (Millipore) according 
to the manufacturer’s instructions. 
2.7. LC–MS/MS analysis 
The enriched peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) 
and directly loaded onto a homemade reversed-phase analytical column 
(15 cm length, 75 μm i.d.). The gradient was increased from 9% to 25% 
(0.1% formic acid in 98% acetonitrile) for 24 min, 25% to 40% in 10 
min, increased to 80% in 3 min, and then held at 80% for 3 min, all at a 
constant flow rate of 800 nL/min, on an EASY-nLC 1000 ultrahigh- 
pressure liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). 
The peptides were subjected to nanospray ionization followed by 
tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) in a Q Exactive™ Plus (Thermo 
Fisher Scientific Inc.) coupled online to an ultrahigh-pressure liquid 
chromatography system. The electrospray voltage applied was 2.0 kV. 
Peptide precursor ions and their secondary fragments were detected and 
analyzed with a high-resolution Orbitrap. The m/z scan range was 350 to 
1800 for full scan, and intact peptides were detected in the Orbitrap at a 
resolution of 70,000. Peptides were then selected for MS/MS using a 
normalized collision energy setting of 30, and the fragments were 
detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 17,500. The data acquisition 
mode used a data-dependent acquisition procedure that alternated be­
tween one mass spectrometry scan and 20 MS/MS scans. The automatic 
gain control was set to 5E4. The signal threshold was set to 5000 ions/s. 
The maximum injection time was set to 200 ms. The dynamic exclusion 
time of MS/MS scanning was set to 15 s to avoid repeated parent ion 
scanning. 
2.8. Database search 
The MS/MS data were processed using the MaxQuant search engine 
(v1.5.2.8, https://www.maxquant.org/) [24]. Tandem mass spectra 
were searched against the Homo_sapiens_9606_SP_20190513 database 
(20,422 sequences). Trypsin/P was specified as a cleavage enzyme 
allowing up to 4 missing cleavages. The minimum length of the peptide 
was 7 amino acid residues, and the maximum number of peptide mod­
ifications was set to 5. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set as 
20 ppm in the first search and 5 ppm in the main search, and the mass 
tolerance for fragment ions was set as 0.02 Da. Fixed modifications were 
specified as carbamidomethylation on cysteine. For methionine oxida­
tion, Kac, Kmal, Ksucc, and Kcr were defined as variable modifications. 
The false discovery rate for protein identification and peptide-spectrum 
matching were adjusted to 1%, and the minimum score for modified 
peptides was set to >40. 
2.9. Bioinformatics analyses 
The models of sequences containing amino acids at specific positions 
of acetyl-21-mers, malonyl-21-mers, crotonyl-21-mers, and succinyl-21- 
mers (ten amino acids upstream and downstream of the site) in all 
protein sequences were analyzed using the online software MoMo with 
the Motif-X algorithm (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/momo) 
[25] and iceLogo [26]. The secondary structure and surface accessibility 
of the amino acids were predicted by NetSurfP software (https://ser 
vices.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetSurfP-3.0/) [27]. The subcellular 
location of the identified proteins was predicted using WoLF PSORT 
(http://www.genscript.com/psort/wolf_psort.html). 
Gene 
Ontology 
(GO)-annotated proteomes were derived from the UniProt-GOA data­
base (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/GOA/) and predicted using InterProScan 
(http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) [28]. The modified proteins were 
classified by GO annotation based on the categories of biological pro­
cess, cellular component, and molecular function. The Kyoto Encyclo­
pedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.genome. 
jp/kegg/) was used to annotate the protein pathways [29]. Inter­
ProScan and the InterPro domain database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/ 
interpro/) were used to annotate protein domain signatures [28]. 
Enrichment analysis was applied based on a two-tailed Fisher’s exact 
test, considering all quantified proteins as the background dataset. A 
corrected p value <0.05 was considered significant. The protein-protein 
interaction information of the studied proteins was retrieved from the 
IntAct molecular interaction database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact/) 
by their gene symbols or STRING software (http://string-db.org/) [30]. 
The results were downloaded in the XGMML format and imported into 
Cytoscape software (http://www.cytoscape.org/, version 3.2.1) to 
visualize and further analyze functional protein-protein interaction 
(PPI) networks. Furthermore, the degree of each protein was calculated 
to evaluate the importance of the protein in the PPI network. 
3. Results 
3.1. Kmal, Ksucc, and Kcr mainly occur in proteins located in the tail of 
human sperm 
In this study, we performed immunoblotting and immunofluores­
cence assays to examine whether human sperm proteins were modified 
by Kmal, Ksucc, and Kcr. Since previous studies have shown that human 
sperm proteins were modified by Kac [12,13], we also examined Kac in 
human sperm as a positive control. A series of acetylated proteins 
(15–150 kDa) were detected in human sperm and were mainly located in 
the tails of human sperm (Fig. 1A), which confirmed previous results 
[12,13]. In addition, malonylated, succinylated, and crotonylated pro­
teins were also detected in human sperm (Fig. 1B–D). Although the 
molecular weights of these modified proteins ranged from 15 kDa to 
150 kDa, the immunoblotting pattern showed respective characteristics 
corresponding to the different PLMs (Fig. 1B–D). Interestingly, the 
modified proteins were mainly located in the sperm tail, predominantly 
in the sperm midpiece. 
3.2. Global mapping of modified proteins and sites in human sperm 
To better characterize the roles of Kac, Kmal, Ksucc and Kcr in 
human sperm, we comprehensively investigated Kac, Kmal, Ksucc and 
Kcr in human sperm by acetylome, malonylome, succinylome and cro­
tonylome analyses using an immunoaffinity approach coupled to LC- 
MS/MS. MS quality control revealed that the length of most peptides 
was between 7 and 20 amino acids, and the mass error of most of the 
spectra was within 10 ppm (Fig. S1), indicating that the sample prepa­
ration and high precision characteristics of MS were met. In total, 1423 
sites in 680 proteins, 196 sites in 118 proteins, 788 sites in 251 proteins, 
and 1836 sites in 645 proteins were identified in the acetylome (Fig. 2A 
and Table S2), malonylome (Fig. 2B and Table S3), succinylome (Fig. 2C

The number of modified sites ranged from 1 to 23 for Kac (Fig. 2E), from 
1 to 17 for Kmal (Fig. 2F), from 1 to 31 for Ksucc (Fig. 2G), and from 1 to

3.3. Sequence properties of modified proteins in human sperm 
To understand the sequence properties of the modified proteins in 
human sperm, we used the Motif-X algorithm to analyze the motif 
characteristics of the modified sites. Among the modified peptides, we 
identified eight motifs for 881 acetylated sites (Fig. 3A and Table S6), 
one motif for 79 succinylated sites (Fig. 3B and Table S7), and 12 motifs 
for 1317 crotonylated sites (Fig. 3C and Table S8). However, no 
conserved motif was identified by Motif-X for Kmal. The consensus se­
quences of the modified proteins were visualized by iceLogo. Nonpolar 
hydrophobic amino acids (glycine, isoleucine, leucine, phenylalanine, 
tryptophan, and valine), polar neutral amino acids (asparagine, serine, 
threonine, and tyrosine), and alkaline amino acids (lysine, arginine, and 
histidine) were clustered near the Kac sites (Fig. S2A). Near the Kmal 
sites, there are nonpolar hydrophobic amino acids (glycine, isoleucine, 
leucine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan), polar neutral amino acids 
(asparagine, serine, and tyrosine), and alkaline amino acids (lysine, 
arginine, and histidine) (Fig. S2B). Nonpolar hydrophobic amino acids 
(alanine, isoleucine, leucine, proline, tryptophan, and valine), polar 
neutral amino acids (asparagine and cystine), and alkaline amino acids 
(lysine, arginine, and histidine) were clustered near the Ksucc sites 
(Fig. S2C). The conserved amino acids near Kcr sites include nonpolar 
hydrophobic amino acids (alanine, glycine, leucine, methionine, 
phenylalanine, and valine), polar neutral amino acids (asparagine, 
glutarnine, and tyrosine), alkaline amino acids (lysine, arginine, and 
histidine), and acidic amino acids (glutamic acid and aspartic acid) 
(Fig. S2D). In addition, the heatmap of the enriched motif neighboring 
the four types of modified sites confirmed the results visualized by ice­
Logo (Fig. S2E–H). Furthermore, the secondary structure preferences of 
the modified proteins were also analyzed. The results showed that 
compared with nonmodified lysine, Kac was more likely to occur in the 
β-strand structure and the surface of the proteins (Fig. 3D), and Kmal 
was also more likely to occur in the β-strand structure (Fig. 3E). How­
ever, no secondary structure preferences of succinylated proteins were 
found (Fig. 3F), while Kcr is unlikely to appear in the β-strand structure 
but is likely to occur in the α-helix and coil structure and the surface of 
the modified proteins (Fig. 3G).

mainly located in the cytoplasm (54%), nucleus (15%), and mitochon­
dria (13%). Succinylated proteins were mainly located in mitochondria 
(64%), the cytoplasm (22%), the nucleus (6%), and extracellular com­
ponents (5%) (Fig. 4C and Table S11). As shown in Fig. 4D and 
Table S12, crotonylated proteins were mainly located in the cytoplasm 
(45%), extracellular components (15%), mitochondria (13%), nucleus 
(13%), and plasma membrane (7%). Gene Ontology (GO) level 2

in biological processes, such as cellular process, single-organism process, 
metabolic process, biological regulation, response to stimulus, cellular 
component organization or biogenesis, localization, and multicellular 
organismal process (Fig. 4E–H and Tables S13–16). According to the 
cellular component annotation, the four modified proteins were mainly 
classified as cell, organelle, membrane-enclosed lumen, extracellular

4. Discussion 
As a hotspot of PTMs, the PLM has been extensively studied in so­
matic cells. In this study, we found that human sperm proteins were 
modified by a classical PLM (Kac) and three novel PLMs (Kmal, Ksucc, 
and Kcr). Similar to those in somatic cells, the modified proteins in 
human sperm were mainly located in the cytoplasm, mitochondria, 
nucleus, and extracellular components and were enriched in diverse 
biological functions and pathways, especially in substance and energy 
metabolism (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5). In addition, these modifications were 
found in 62 male infertility-associated proteins (Fig. 8) and could serve 
as potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for male 
infertility. 
Kac is one of the most prominent PLMs and serves as a key regulatory 
mechanism in all eukaryotic organisms. The acetylome of human sperm 
was revealed approximately ten years ago by Sun et al. (2014) and Yu 
et al. (2015) [12,13]. Sun et al. (2014) identified 1206 Kac sites in 576 
proteins [12], while Yu et al. (2015) detected 973 Kac sites in 456 
proteins [13]. According to our previous acetylome data, 321 new Kac 
proteins were discovered in human sperm, while 359 reported Kac 
proteins were identified. In addition, 326 Kac sites in 174 acetylated 
proteins were also identified in our study (Table S2), while 1097 new 
Kac sites in 506 proteins were found in our study (Table S2). Similar to 
previous acetylome data, our data demonstrated that the acetylated 
proteins in human sperm are also enriched in substance and energy 
metabolism, such as the citrate cycle, glycolysis, and oxidative phos­
phorylation (Fig. 5I). In addition, Kac sites in male infertility-associated 
proteins, including dynein axonemal heavy chain 1 (DNAH1), cilia and 
flagella associated protein 43 (CFAP43), A-kinase anchoring protein 4 
(AKAP4), and outer dense fiber of sperm tails 1 (ODF1), were also found 
in previous studies [12,13]. Interestingly, we found some new Kac sites 
in male infertility-associated proteins, including dynein axonemal heavy 
chain 2 (DNAH2), dynein axonemal heavy chain 7 (DNAH7), radial 
spoke head component 9 (RSPH9), and meiosis-specific nuclear struc­
tural 1 (MNS1) (Table S2). Therefore, the present study deepens the 
research on Kac in human sperm and may lay the foundation for using 
Kac as a diagnostic treatment for male infertility in the future. 
Kmal is a novel PLMs which regulates diverse biological processes 
and physiological processes. It is involved in metabolic diseases, 
inflammation, and tumorigenesis [31]. The malonylome of mouse livers 
identified 1137 Kmal sites in 430 proteins that are enriched in cellular 
metabolic networks, such as fatty acid β-oxidation, glycolysis, and the 
urea cycle [21]. In addition, 4943 Kmal sites were recognized on 1822 
proteins in human fibroblasts [32]. However, in this study, we detected 
fewer malonylated proteins in human sperm than in mouse liver and 
human fibroblasts (Fig. 2B). Malonylated proteins in human sperm are 
also enriched in glycolysis and fatty acid β-oxidation (Fig. 5J). Inter­
estingly, some testis-specific isoforms of enzymes involved in glycolysis, 
such as glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, spermatogenesis 
(GAPDHS), phosphoglycerate kinase 2 (PGK2), and lactate dehydroge­
nase C (LDHC), are modified by Kmal (Table S3). Furthermore, this is the 
first report showing that many male infertility-associated proteins are 
modified by Kmal (Fig. 8). These results suggest that Kmal in human 
sperm may affect the energy metabolism that is essential for human 
sperm and may be involved in the pathogenesis of male infertility. 
In recent years, Ksucc has attracted increased interest among re­
searchers since it participates in many pathophysiological processes. 
The quantitative global succinylome in human cell lines and tissues 
revealed more than thousands of Ksucc sites. The modified proteins were 
enriched in pathways related to substance and energy metabolism [33]. 
In this study, we characterized the first succinylome in human sperm. 
Equivalent numbers of Ksucc sites were identified in human sperm 
compared to those in somatic cells (Fig. 2C). Similar to those in somatic 
cells, the modified human sperm proteins were located mainly in the 
mitochondria and cytoplasm and were enriched in substances and en­
ergy metabolism, such as the citrate cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, 
glycolysis, and fatty acid β-oxidation (Fig. 4C and Fig. 5K). Interestingly, 
some testis-specific isoforms of enzymes involved in these metabolic 
pathways, such as GAPDHS, LDHC, and pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 
subunit alpha 2 (PDHA2), are modified by Ksucc (Table S4). Further­
more, this is the first study in which proteins associated with astheno­
zoospermia, spermatogenic failure, and abnormal acrosome and 
globozoospermia were modified by Ksucc (Fig. 8). These results may 
provide potential targets for the diagnosis of male infertility. 
Kcr, a novel PTM, has been reported to play an important role in 
normal physiological functions and disease development, such as 
gametogenesis and cancer [34,35]. Kcr was first discovered in gene 
transcription promoter regions and enhancers and promotes gene 
expression. Subsequent studies have shown that Kcr has multiple func­
tions in germ cells [36]. Kcr can regulate the proliferation and apoptosis 
of human granulosa cells, thereby affecting the maturation of oocytes

[37]. Moreover, the level of histone Kcr can also serve as an indicator of 
gene expression in male germ cells in sperm and can help sperm avoid 
gene transcription silencing during the process of sperm development 
and maturation [38]. In this study, we characterized the first crotony­
lome in human sperm. Many of the modified proteins were also found in 
a previous study [39]. These proteins were enriched in the ribosome and 
proteasome pathways (Fig. 5L). These results suggest that Kcr may have 
a regulatory effect on protein synthesis during spermatogenesis. In 
addition, we found that many proteins in energy pathways were modi­
fied by Kcr, indicating the role of Kcr in the regulation of sperm energy 
metabolism (Fig. 5L). Furthermore, among the 62 modified proteins 
associated with male infertility, the most frequent modification was Kcr 
(Fig. 8). This result suggested the functional role of Kcr in male 
reproduction. 
After analyzing the data of the four PLMs, we believe that all four 
PLMs may regulate the function of human sperm through energy 
metabolism pathways, but their focus is different. We speculate that Kac 
has an impact on multiple energy metabolism pathways, Kmal has a 
significant impact on glycolysis, and Ksucc has a significant impact on 
oxidative phosphorylation and ATP generation within mitochondria. 
The characteristics of Kcr are similar to those of Kac, as it has regulatory 
effects on various energy metabolism pathways. In addition, Kcr plays 
an important role in protein generation and degradation. 
To further understand the role of these four modifications in male 
fertility, we performed a bibliographic search and identified 62 modified 
proteins associated with male infertility, including asthenozoospermia, 
spermatogenic failure, abnormal acrosome and globozoospermia, 
hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and polycystic kidney disease 
(Fig. 8). Among these proteins, MMAF- and PCD-related proteins 
maintain the structure of the sperm flagella. The pathogenesis of MMAF 
and PCD is closely associated with gene mutations [40]. The modifica­
tions of MMAF and PCD-related proteins may change the structure of 
these proteins to regulate the formation of sperm flagella and sperm 
morphology and motility [40]. In addition, the modifications of other 
proteins associated with male infertility may affect the structure, func­
tion and/or stability of these proteins to regulate male reproduction. 
These results may provide potential targets for the diagnosis and treat­
ment of male infertility in the future. However, further studies should be 
performed to validate these findings. 
In somatic cells, histone modification is an important epigenetic 
event, while in germ cells, most histones are replaced by protamine 
during spermatogenesis [41]. The transformation from histone to prot­
amine is very important for sperm and has a great impact on male 
fertility [42]. The highly acetylated histones in mammalian testes can 
promote the excretion of histones and improve the succession of prot­
amines [43]. Kcr of histones is also indispensable in the process of his­
tone replacement [44]. In this study, we found that human sperm 
histones are frequently modified by Kac and Kcr (Fig. S4). Interestingly, 
we also detected Ksucc in histone H2B type 1-A and Kmal in histone H2B 
type 1-L in human sperm, suggesting a possible role for Ksucc and Kmal 
in histone replacement (Fig. S4). 
Microtubules assemble axonemes, which are the core structures of 
sperm flagella. Tubulins are key components of microtubules and 
regulate their properties and functions [45]. Tubulins undergo a series 
of PTMs that modulate their depolymerization and assembly. Acetyla­
tion of tubulin promotes tubulin assembly and prevents tubulin depo­
lymerization [46]. Knockout of the genes encoding enzymes that modify 
tubulin PTMs resulted in a phenotype of asthenoteratozoospermia- 
MMAF [45]. In this study, Kac was found in α-tubulin and β-tubulin in 
human sperm, consistent with previous studies [45,47]. Interestingly, 
this study is the first to report the Kmal and Ksucc of α-tubulins and Kcr 
of α-tubulins and β-tubulins in human sperm (Fig. S4). These results 
suggest that the PLMs of tubulins may regulate tubulin depolymeriza­
tion and assembly to modulate the formation of sperm flagella. 
5. Conclusion 
In this study, four PLMs (Kac, Kmal, Ksucc, and Kcr) were detected 
among human sperm proteins. Global characterization of these four 
PLMs identified thousands of modified sites in hundreds of proteins that 
participate in a variety of biological processes and metabolic pathways. 
Crosstalk analysis showed that the modified proteins in sperm energy 
pathways and male fertility-related proteins were involved in a PPI 
network associated with male reproduction. Our findings provide a 
reference for studying the pathophysiological function of human sperm 
PLMs and lay the foundation for using PLMs for the diagnosis and 
treatment of male infertility.
