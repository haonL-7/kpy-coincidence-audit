Neurodegeneration via Suppressing Histone Acetylation by ANP32A

Abstract
Homocysteine (Hcy) is an independent and serious risk factor for dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), but the 
precise mechanisms are still poorly understood. In the current study, we observed that the permissive histone mark trimethyl 
histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4me3) and its methyltransferase KMT2B were significantly elevated in hyperhomocysteinemia 
(HHcy) rats, with impairment of synaptic plasticity and cognitive function. Further research found that histone methylation 
inhibited synapse-associated protein expression, by suppressing histone acetylation. Inhibiting H3K4me3 by downregulat-
ing KMT2B could effectively restore Hcy-inhibited H3K14ace in N2a cells. Moreover, chromatin immunoprecipitation 
revealed that Hcy-induced H3K4me3 resulted in ANP32A mRNA and protein overexpression in the hippocampus, which 
was regulated by increased transcription Factor c-fos and inhibited histone acetylation and synapse-associated protein expres-
sion, and downregulating ANP32A could reverse these changes in Hcy-treated N2a cells. Additionally, the knockdown of 
KMT2B restored histone acetylation and synapse-associated proteins in Hcy-treated primary hippocampal neurons. These 
data have revealed a novel crosstalk mechanism between KMT2B-H3K4me3-ANP32A-H3K14ace, shedding light on its 
role in Hcy-related neurogenerative disorders.

homocysteine directly causes neurodegeneration and the 
underlying mechanisms are unclear.
Regional brain atrophy is a feature of neurodegenera-
tion and a hallmark of AD [3]. Prospective studies have 
reported links between elevated Hcy and elevated global 
or regional atrophy rates in individuals with mild cog-
nitive impairment (MCI) [4, 5]. Moreover, there was a 
concentration-related association of Hcy with cognitive 
impairment: the higher the concentration, the faster the 
cognition deficit [6]. A longitudinal study reported that 
elevated plasma Hcy was associated with a growing risk 
of cognitive deficits in several domains 7 years later [7]. 
An early study indicated that the AD estimated population 
attributable risk to Hcy was 16% [8]. Overall, these pro-
spective studies found that increased Hcy is a prominent 
risk factor for incident dementia or cognitive deficits.
Histone modification is a significant mechanism of 
epigenetic regulation that influences gene expression, 
particularly in aging and neurodegeneration [9]. Specific 
modifications of histone proteins can lead to chromatin 
remodeling, ultimately controlling gene expression pat-
terns. Recent studies indicate that the histone methylation 
induced by the elevated Hcy is a mechanism observed 
in various diseases, such as cancer, atherosclerosis, 
and aging [10–12]. Impaired synaptic plasticity is one 
of the essential mechanisms of cognitive impairment in 
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), which could be regulated by 
histone acetylation [13, 14]. Recent pathological studies 
have highlighted reduced histone acetylation and elevated 
histone methylation’s critical roles in memory [15–17]. 
However, whether the crosstalk mechanism between his-
tone methylation and acetylation for Hcy-induced synap-
tic damage remains unclear.
In the present study, we identified remarkable cogni-
tive dysfunction and decreased expression of synapse-
associated proteins accompanied by the significantly 
elevated permissive histone mark trimethyl histone H3 
lysine 4 (H3K4me3) and its methyltransferase KMT2B in 
Hcy-treated rats. Among the upregulated genes induced 
by Hcy, ANP32A, which binds to histones to suppress 
histone acetylation, was significantly increased in Hcy-
treated N2a cells and rats. Knockdown of KMT2B and 
ANP32A resulted in restoring histone acetylation levels 
and synapse-associated proteins in Hcy-treated N2a cells. 
Moreover, knocking down KMT2B led to the recovery of 
synaptic plasticity and cognitive function in primary hip-
pocampal neurons. These data revealed a novel epigenetic 
mechanism, KMT2B-H3K4me3-ANP32A-H3K14ace, 
which is crucial in Hcy-induced neurodegeneration.
Material and Methods
Animals and Drug Administration
Sprague‒Dawley rats (8-week-old, male) were 
obtained from SiBeiFu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., 
Ltd. This experimentation was authorized by the Ani-
mal Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University (JN. 
No20211115S0450406[406]). SD rats were housed indi-
vidually with free access to water and food and were main-
tained on a reverse 12:12-h light/dark cycle. For a dura-
tion of 21 days, the rats received vena caudalis injections 
of either saline or Hcy (400 µg/kg/day, purchased from 
Sigma, USA), with or without simultaneous administration 
of folate (4 mg/kg/day, obtained from Sigma, USA) and 
vitamin B12 (250 g/kg/day, obtained from Sigma, USA) 
dissolved in drinking water. All rats were divided into the 
Con group (saline injection), the Hcy group (Hcy injec-
tion), and the Hcy + FB group (Hcy injection and supple-
mentation with folate and vitamin B12). The injection was 
administered daily from 8:30 am to 11:00 am. Their spatial 
memory was measured during the final week, after which 
the animals were killed within 24 h [18].
Morris Water Maze (MWM)
During the training, we randomly selected one of the four 
starting positions (south, north, east, or west) and recorded 
the time until the rat discovered the hidden platform. If 
the time was over 1 min, the rats were gently guided to 
find the hidden platform and kept on the platform for 30 
s. Each rat underwent four training sessions per day for 
six consecutive training days,, with a 15- to 20-min break 
between each session. On Day 7, the hidden platform was 
taken out of the pool. The escape latency, crossing time, 
proportion of time spent in the intended quadrant, swim 
distance, and speed in 1 min were measured using the 
MWM system (WMT-100S, Chengdu Techman Software 
Co. Ltd., China).
Golgi Staining
After anesthetization with 20% urethane, the rats were 
perfused with sodium nitrite normal saline for 30 min 
and 4% formaldehyde normal saline tissue fixative for 1 
h as previously described [19]. Next, the brain was per-
fused with Golgi fixative and incubated in dyeing solution 
in situ away from light according to a previous method 
[20]. After treatment for 1 week, the brain was cut into 
40-µm-thick slices by a vibrating microtome (VT 1200S;

Electrophysiology
As previously mentioned [21], the rat brains were cut into 
400-µm-thick slices via a vibrating microtome (VT 1200S; 
Leica, Germany). After these slices were incubated in oxy-

stimulating the Schaffer collateral pathway using MED64 
software (Alpha MED Sciences). The first 30 min of the 
experiment were used to establish baseline responses. Given 
high-frequency stimuli (HFS) at 100 Hz for 1 s, the fEPSP 
response was observed for 2 h.
Plasma Homocysteine Concentration Assayed 
by ELISA Kit
Blood naturally coagulated at room temperature for about 
15 min, and the supernatant was carefully collected after 
centrifugation at 2000–3000 rpm for 20 min. The 96-well

contained 50 µl of sample and 100 µl of horseradish per-
oxidase (HRP)–labeled detection antibody. The wells were 
sealed and incubated at room temperature for about 60 min. 
After that, the well was cleaned with washing solution for 60 
s/3 times, and then 50 µl of substrates A and B was added.

and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min. The OD val-
ues were detected at a wavelength of 450 nm by a multifunc-
tional microplate detector (Synergy H4, USA). The sample 
concentration was calculated based on a standard curve.
Western Blot

analysis by the BCA protein quantification kit E112 (Novo-
zan Co., Ltd., CN). Equal 20 µg samples were loaded for

was incubated with the membranes at room temperature for

Brain Slice Preparation
After rats were anesthetized using urethane (0.1 ml/10 g), 
the brain was perfused with 100 ml NS and 400 ml 4% para-
formaldehyde solution. Brains were cut into 12-µm-thick 
slices for immunofluorescence using a freezing microtome 
(Leica). The brain slices were applied. Then, the fixed brain 
tissue was immersed in 10% (1 day), 20% (1 day), and 30% 
(3 days) sucrose in PBS at 4 °C. Coronal brain slabs con-
taining the hippocampus were cut into 6-µm-thick slices for 
Nissl staining after paraffin embedding.
Nissl Staining
After being embedded in paraffin, the brain blocks were 
deparaffinized by rehydration in xylene and ethanol con-
centrations ranging from 50 to 100% and then washed in 
water. The blocks were stained with 0.1% (w/v) cresyl violet 
and observed by light microscopy. Images from the complete 
hippocampus, CA1, CA3, and DG were recorded, and the 
neuron morphology and arrangement per field were ana-
lyzed. Quantitative analysis of images was used by ImageJ 
software.
Immunofluorescence
The cultured cells were then permeabilized in 0.5% Triton 
X-100 (v/v) diluted in PBS solution after fixation in 4% para-
formaldehyde (v/v) at room temperature for 30 min. The 
cells were incubated in 5% BSA (w/v) dissolved in 0.1% 
Triton X-100 (v/v) for 30 min. The brain slices or cultured 
cells were treated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, 
washed 3 times with PBS, and then incubated for another 
hour at 37 °C with secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa 
Fluor 594. The samples were further stained with an antifad-
ing mounting medium (with DAPI) for 10 min. Microscopy 
was used to detect the tissue or cell morphology (Carl LSM 
780, Zeiss, Germ; LSM880, Zeiss, Germ). Quantitative 
analysis of images was used by ImageJ software.
Transmission Electron Microscopy
Electron microscopy was used to assess ultrastructural 
alterations in the hippocampus. After completing the water 
maze test, the rats were euthanized, and then their hearts 
were transfused with 3% paraformaldehyde and 2% glu-
taraldehyde dissolved in PBS. Hippocampal sections were 
commissioned by the Shiyanjia lab (www.​shiya​njia.​com). A 
transmission electron microscope (H-7650, Hitachi, Japan) 
was used for observation.
Reverse Transcription and Real‑Time Quantitative 
PCR (RT‒PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from the hippocampus of rats by 
TRIzol reagent (Cowin Bio, Beijing, China). According to 
the instructions of Novozan kits (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.), 
the genomic DNA was removed from the sample at 42 °C for 
5 min and reverse transcribed at 50 °C for 15 min and 85 °C 
for 5S. A 20 µl PCR system was detected by an RT‒PCR 
Cycler (Lightcycler480II, Roche Diagnostics International 
Ltd., Switzerland). The detailed conditions were the same 
as in the previous experiment [15]. The comparative CT 
method was used to quantify the expression levels of genes 
of interest compared with GAPDH for normalization. The 
corresponding primer sequences are shown in Table 2.
Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Assay
Hippocampal tissues were pulverized in a lysis buffer con-
taining proteinase (Cocktail, Roche) and phosphatase inhibi-
tors after cross-linking with 1% formaldehyde. Then, the 
mixtures were subjected to sonication at a power of 25% for 
15 s, 10 cycles with 45 s intervals. The aliquot sample of the 
sheared chromatin was used as input. The remaining por-
tions were divided between immunoprecipitation using anti-
H4K12ace, anti-H3K14ace, anti-H3K4me3 antibody or IgG, 
and overnight incubation with protein A + G agarose beads 
(Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The mixtures 
were centrifuged and sequentially washed with low-salt 
buffer, high-salt buffer, and LiCl buffer. After eluting and 
centrifuging to remove the supernatant, the samples were 
vortexed at 30 ℃ for 15 min, and then 5 M NaCl (4.8 µl) and 
10 mg/ml RNase A (2 µl) were added. For reversing cross-
linking, the samples were vortexed and incubated at 65 ℃ 
overnight, and 20 mg/ml proteinase K (2 µl) was added at 60 
℃ for 1 h. After isolation by phenol/chloroform extraction, 
DNA was resuspended in distilled ­H2O.
Real-time PCR (Lightcycler480II, Roche Diagnostics 
International Ltd., Switzerland) was performed. Cycling 
conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 
cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, 
and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation, 
the DNA suspension was collected to perform an agarose 
gel electrophoresis experiment for semi-quantitative analy-
sis. Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories) was used to 
quantitatively examine the result. The primers targeting the 
promoter or coding regions of the genes are listed in Table 2.

Results
Hcy Induced Brain Atrophy and Cognitive Deficits 
in Rats
To determine the influence of treatment with Hcy for 3 
weeks on cognitive function in Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats, 
we first examined spatial memory capacity changes by the 
MWM test, plasma Hcy level, and brain weight after 3 
weeks of Hcy injection by tail vein (Fig. 1A–C). We found 
that the level of plasma Hcy increased to approximately 12 
µmol/l, and supplementation with folate and vitamin B12 
reversed the changes (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, the mean brain 
weight of the Hcy group was approximately 1.75 g, which 
was significantly less than that of the Con group, and treat-

induced by Hcy (Fig. 1C).
The effect of Hcy on cognition was determined by the 
Morris water maze (MWM) test. During the continuous 6 
days of training, the statistical results showed that the Hcy 
group had a remarkably longer escape latency than the Con 
group on the 2nd, 4th, 5th, and 6th days. In contrast, the 
escape latency of the Hcy + FB group was faster than that of 
the Hcy group on the 3rd, 4th, 5th, and 6th days (Fig. 1D). 
Spatial memory was also detected after removing the under-
water platform on Day 7, which suggested cognitive impair-

B12 efficiently attenuated cognitive impairment (Fig. 1E–J). 
The escape latency of the first crossing in the platform area 
was significantly increased in the Hcy group, while the 
Hcy + FB group efficiently reduced the escape latency on 
Day 7 (Fig. 1F). Meanwhile, in memory testing, the cross-
ing times in 1 min and the time spent in the target quadrant 
were decreased in the Hcy group compared with the Con 
group, while the Hcy + FB group displayed an increase in 
both parameters on Day 7 (Fig. 1G, H). There were no obvi-

groups on Day 7 (Fig. 1I, J). These results revealed that Hcy 
could impair cognitive function, and treatment with folate 
and vitamin B12 effectively attenuated cognitive impairment 
in Hcy-treated rats.

To investigate the impact of Hcy on synapse morphology, 
we measured the dendritic arbors in the hippocampal CA1 
and DG regions through Golgi staining and concentric circle 
analysis. We also refined the effects of Hcy on the forma-
tion of dendritic spines by morphological analysis (Fig. 2A). 
We observed a significant decrease in spine density in CA1 
and DG neurons of Hcy-treated rats compared with that of 
control rats (Fig. 2B, C). We further analyzed the subtype 
of dendritic spines based on head and neck size [23]. Both 
mushroom and thin spines were decreased in CA1 and DG 
neurons of Hcy-treated rats compared with control rats, indi-

reduced in Hcy-treated rats (Fig. 2D, E). We observed that 
Hcy-treated rats had shorter dendritic lengths (Fig. S1A-D) 
and fewer dendritic crossings (Fig. S1E-F) in the CA1 and

effect of Hcy on the long-term potentiation (LTP) of hip-

Hcy did not significantly injure presynaptic function. Subse-
quently, the slope of fEPSP was significantly reduced in the 
Hcy-treated group compared with that in the Con group, and 
folate and vitamin B12 attenuated the impairments (Fig. 2H,

Neurofibrillary tangles accumulated by hyperphosphoryl-
ated tau are a classic pathologic hallmark in the brain of AD. 
There was a strong relationship between plasma Hcy and 
the density of neurofibrillary tangles [48]. Further studies 
found that Hcy can directly activate Cdk5 and increase tau 
phosphorylation [49]. On the other hand, Hcy plays a vital 
role in the process of β-amyloid formation, which is another 
hallmark of AD [50]. Although there is a large amount of 
evidence to confirm the involvement of Hcy in AD patho-
logical formation, it is currently unclear whether Hcy could 
directly induce neurodegeneration.
Cortex atrophy is not only an important feature of neu-
rodegeneration but also a pathological manifestation of 
AD. Several cross-sectional studies have shown that the 
average medial temporal lobe thickness decreased by 19 to 
32% in AD patients whose plasma Hcy level exceeded 11.1 
µmol/l [47, 51, 52]. This Hcy-induced atrophy has also been 
observed in elderly individuals [53].
Epigenetic modification of chromatin, including his-
tone acetylation and methylation, plays an important role 
in neurodevelopment, neurodegeneration and aging [26]. 
Histone methylation is usually located at the lysine or argi-
nine N-terminal region, which induces the suppression or 
activation of gene expression. Each lysine residue can be 
mono-, di-, and tri-methylated, and the residues can form 
different methylation combinations [54, 55]. Histone meth-
ylation and demethylation are catalyzed by HMTs or his-
tone demethylases (HMDs), respectively, which alter the 
structure of chromatin to prevent or facilitate access of tran-
scription factors to genomic DNA, regulating gene repres-
sion or expression in a complex manner [56]. HMTs that 
catalyze the methylation of lysine amino acids of histones 
are called lysine methyltransferases (KMTs). Methylation at 
lysine in histone H3 can serve to repress and activate gene 
transcription. H3K4 methylation is associated with active 
gene expression. H3K4me3 is found in nucleosomes associ-
ated with the promoter regions of actively transcribed genes 
[57]. Recently, studies have found that H3K4me3 is signifi-
cantly increased in human AD postmortem brain tissues and 
AD mouse models [58, 59]. Although it was reported that 
increased H3K4me3 can inhibit the expression of learning-
related proteins, the precise mechanism of inhibition is still 
be determined, especially the mechanism of histone acetyla-
tion regulation, which has yet to been reported.
It was reported that there is a prominent link between 
mutations in H3K4 demethylases or methyltransferases and 
disease with cognitive deficits [60, 61]. In addition, KMT2A, 
KMT2B, and SETD1A are also increased in AD brains [62]. 
Here, we demonstrated that the mRNA and protein levels 
of H3K4me3, a histone mark for gene activation, and its 
methyltransferase KMT2B were significantly increased in 
Hcy-treated N2a cells and rats.
In addition to histone methylation, histone acetylation 
plays a critical and direct role in regulating genes for learn-
ing and memory in the nervous system [63]. Histone acetyla-
tion could diminish the affinity between histone and synaptic 
protein-related genes that allow for transcription [38]. The 
impairment of histone acetylation is causally linked to neu-
rodegeneration and cognitive impairment, and the upregula-
tion of histones could rescue synapse and memory deficits 
[21]. In the current study, we observed that Hcy decreased 
H3K14 and H4K12 levels in the brains of rats and N2a cells.
Deacetylation and acetylation of histones are promoted by 
deacetylases (HDACs) and acetyltransferases (HATs). Here, 
we found that the expression levels of HDAC1, HDAC3, 
and ANP32A were significantly elevated in the Hcy-treated 
rats, but only ANP32A was effectively improving after 
being treated with folate and VitB12. Histone acetylation 
is also suppressed by ANP32A, which can bind to histones 
and mask their access to HATs [64, 65]. Recent research, 
including ours, demonstrated that ANP32A was not only 
upregulated in the AD brain and AD animal model but also 
in sevoflurane-induced postoperative cognitive dysfunction 
[20, 21, 66, 67]. Indeed, our previous research demonstrated 
that AD risk factors, including Aβ1-42, ­H2O2, human tau, 
and an anesthetic (sevoflurane), could increase ANP32 gene 
transcription, leading to cognitive impairment. These data 
indicated that ANP32A upregulation can serve to repress 
gene transcription, particularly during memory processes, 
especially in neurodegenerative processes.
Our ChIP experiments revealed that the anp32a gene had 
increased H3K4me3 occupancy at its promoter, which may 
be responsible for ANP32A upregulation in Hcy-treated rats. 
In agreement with the increased transcription of anp32a in 
RT‒PCR data, ANP32A was elevated in the hippocampus 
of Hcy-treated rats. These data suggested that ANP32A 
upregulated by H3K4me3 binds and inhibits histone acety-
lation, leading to neurodegeneration.
KMT2B, the histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) tri-methyltrans-
ferase, is conserved throughout evolution, and its upregula-
tion is found in AD [37]. Here, we provide evidence that 
upregulated H3K4me3 inhibited histone acetylation via 
ANP32A. We further demonstrate that this inhibitory effect 
could influence H3K14 and H4K12, which play critical roles 
in synapse plasticity and are reduced in AD.

In the present study, we identified that the permissive his-
tone mark H3K4me3 and its methyltransferase KTM2B are 
significantly elevated in hyperhomocysteinemia rats. Knock-
down of KTM2B leads to recovery of synaptic plasticity and 
cognitive function. Among the upregulated genes induced by 
Hcy, ANP32A, which binds to histones to suppress histone 
acetylation, was significantly increased in Hcy-treated cells 
and rats. Knockdown of ANP32A restores histone acetyla-
tion and synapse-associated proteins in primary hippocam-
pal neurons. Taken together, Hcy induced synaptic damage 
via the KMT2B-H3K4me3-ANP32A-H3K14ace epigenetic 
mechanism.
