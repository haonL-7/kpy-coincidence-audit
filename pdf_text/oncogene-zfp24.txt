ARTICLE
Crosstalk between SUMOylation and ubiquitination controls the
stability of transcription factor zinc ﬁnger protein 24: a novel
antitumor mechanism in bladder cancer
Xiaosong Wei

Zinc ﬁnger protein 24 (ZNF24) is a conserved multifunctional transcription factor associated with tumorigenesis, but its function in
bladder carcinogenesis remains unclear. Herein, the expression of ZNF24 was decreased in bladder cancer (BC) cells and tissues, and
patients with higher expression of ZNF24 had a better prognosis. Doxycycline-induced overexpression and knockdown of ZNF24
identiﬁed its anti-proliferative and anti-metastasis role in BC in vitro and in vivo. The potential genes for the anti-cancer role of ZNF24,
involving transcriptional regulation of several factors, such as dual-speciﬁcity phosphatase 1 and squalene epoxidase. E2 conjugating
enzyme UBC9 and small ubiquitin-like modiﬁer (SUMO) 1 were found to interact with ZNF24, suggesting that ZNF24 may be
SUMOylated. Consistent with the expression, ZNF24 SUMOylation levels were decreased in BC cells and tissues. Pan-SUMOylation
inhibition promoted protein degradation of ZNF24. UBC9 SUMOylated ZNF24 at Lys-27 (K27) site with SUMO1 modiﬁcation and the
K27 mutation of ZNF24 greatly damaged the protein stability of ZNF24. Cullin 3 (CUL3), a E3 ubiquitin ligase, was responsible for the
degradation of ZNF24. ZNF24 SUMOylation prevented CUL3-mediated protein degradation of ZNF24. Overall, the crosstalk between
the SUMOylation and ubiquitination of ZNF24 may be a novel regulatory mechanism to block tumorigenesis and development of BC.

INTRODUCTION
Bladder cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy of the
urinary system, characterized by high morbidity and mortality [1].
Although a variety of treatments, including surgery, intravesical
chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, can improve the prognosis
of patients with BC, the recurrence rate within 5 years is still high
[2, 3]. Therefore, new treatments are sorely needed.
SUMOylation is a reversible post-translational modiﬁcation that
is present in almost all eukaryotes [4]. SUMOylation contains a
conserved catalytic cascade—small ubiquitin-like modiﬁer (SUMO)
molecule maturation, activation, UBC9-SUMO molecule conjuga-
tion, SUMO molecule-substrate ligation, and SUMO molecule-
substrate separation (de-modiﬁcation) [5]. With the help of the E2
enzyme ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 9 (UBC9), SUMO proteins
are covalently bound to lysine (K) residues of the target protein,
altering the stability, subcellular localization, or binding afﬁnity of
a wide range of proteins [5]. Xia et al. quantiﬁed the SUMOylation
pattern in BC patients and reported that a high SUMO score (to a
certain extent, representing the level of SUMOylation) was
associated with poor prognosis and low chemotherapy sensitivity
in BC patients, suggesting that SUMOylation promotes the
progression of BC [6]. However, several researchers also demon-
strated the cancer-inhibiting role of SUMOylation in BC. They
found that sentrin/SUMO-speciﬁc protease3 (SENP3) was highly
expressed in BC tissues and promoted BC progression through
deSUMOylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription
3 (STAT3) [7]. UBC9 was overexpressed in BC tissues but was
negatively correlated with TNM stage and grade [8]. Therefore,
simply studying the function of SUMOylation may be one-sided
and should be based on the function of the target protein.
Currently, only a few factors such as heterogeneous nuclear
ribonucleoprotein A1, DExD-box helicase 39B, Δ24-dehydrocho-
lesterol reductase, and STAT3 were reported to be SUMOylated in
BC [9–11]. Revealing the role of more SUMOylated proteins in BC
may provide a new perspective for targeted therapy of BC.
The Kruppel-Like Factor family of regulatory proteins, which
contains 18 members, is transcription factors [12, 13]. Their
functions cover a variety of diseases, including carcinogenesis,
liver ﬁbrosis, and metabolic syndrome [12]. Zinc ﬁnger protein 24
(ZNF24) is a member of the Kruppel-like zinc ﬁnger transcription
factor family and has the contradictory function in different
cancers [13]. Jia et al. reported that ZNF24 acted as a cancer
suppressor in breast cancer by inhibiting angiogenesis [14].
However, in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, ZNF24 was
signiﬁcantly overexpressed in cancer tissues, and its knockdown
suppressed the proliferation of Hep3B cells [15]. At present, the
function of ZNF24 in BC is unknown. The expression and
prognostic value of ZNF24 in BC patients were analyzed by using
the TNMplot and Kaplan-Meier Plotter databases, and showed that
compared with non-cancer tissues, ZNF24 was signiﬁcantly
reduced in BC tissues, and patients with higher expression of
ZNF24 had better survival rate. The contradictory function of
ZNF24 in cancer progression and its abnormal expression in BC
progression prompted us to explore its function in BC.
SUMOylation is reported to regulate the interaction between
modiﬁed proteins and other proteins and mediates the localiza-
tion, functional regulation, and degradation of target proteins [16].
Proteins, such as meiotic recombination 11 homolog 1 and p63,
undergo SUMOylation and their ubiquitination modiﬁcation
weakens, thereby avoiding recognition and degradation by
proteases [17, 18]. Results of database analysis (JASSA and GPS-
SUMO websites) showed that there was a high conﬁdence
SUMOylation site at K27 of ZNF24. Whether the SUMOylation at
this site affects the stability and ubiquitination of ZNF24 deserves
further investigation. The factors related to the regulation of
ZNF24 SUMOylation were explored by IP-LC/MS assay.
The C terminal of ZNF24 contains four C2H2 zinc ﬁnger motifs,
which are DNA binding domains and essential for regulating
downstream gene transcription [19, 20]. As a transcription factor,
ZNF24 is known to promote the transcription of downstream
genes, such as wnt family member 8B, and can also inhibit the
transcription of downstream genes, such as vascular endothelial
growth factor [14, 21]. Herein, chromatin immunoprecipitation
sequencing (ChIP-seq) combined with mRNA transcriptomic
sequencing (mRNA-seq) were conducted to explore the potential
mechanism of ZNF24 regulating the progression of BC.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Tissue samples
The 20 pairs of BC and adjacent non-tumor tissues and BC parafﬁn tissue
sections from 78 patients were collected from the First Afﬁliated Hospital
of Zhengzhou University.
Cell culture
Human ureteral epithelial cell line (SV-HUC-1), BC cell lines (T24, J82, 5637,
TCCSUP, and HT-1376), and 293 T cell line were purchased from iCell
Biotechnology, Inc (Shanghai, China). SV-HUC-1 cells were grown in F12K
medium (iCell). T24 cells were maintained in McCOY’s 5 A medium
(Servicebio, Wuhan, China). J82, TCCSUP, and HT-1376 cells were placed in
MEM medium (Solarbio, Beijing, China). 5637 cells were grown in RPMI-
1640 medium (Solarbio). 293 T cells were maintained in DMEM medium
(Servicebio). All mediums were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum
(FBS, Tianhang Biotechnology, Zhejiang, China) and placed in an incubator
at 37 °C with 5% CO2.
Lentivirus production
Dox-inducible lentiviral vectors were used to interfere with the expression
of ZNF24 in CRC cells. For ZNF24 silencing, empty vector Tet-pLKO-EGFP
system was used as the control. The shRNA speciﬁcally targeting ZNF24
mRNA in CDS region or 3ʹ-untranslated regions (3ʹ-UTR) was inserted into
Tet-pLKO-EGFP lentivirus vector to obtain the recombinant Tet-pLKO-
EGFP-shZNF24 vector. To prepare lentivirus for introduction of shRNAs into
CRC cells, HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with recombinant Tet-pLKO-
EGFP-shZNF24, pSPAX2, and pMD2.G vectors with the help of Lipofecta-
mine 3000. Subsequently, CRC cells were infected with lentivirus particle to
induce ZNF24 silencing in CRC cells. Cells infected with lentiviruses were
then selected by 2 μg/mL puromycin to generate stable cell clones. Two
ZNF24-targeting sequences were used:
shRNA sequences targeting the 3ʹ-UTR: ccgggtttgaaagaaagagaaatcttcaa
gagagatttctctttctttcaaaccttttt
shRNA sequences targeting the CDS region: ccggaagaagattcaatacttatctt-
caagagagataagtattgaatcttcttcttttt. The viral titer for the Tet-pLKO-EGFP
system was 2 × 108 TU/mL. To induce ZNF24 silencing, cells were treated
with 1 μg/mL Dox at the time point indicated.
For ZNF24 overexpression, empty vector pLVX-TetOne-EGFP system
served as a control. The CDS region of ZNF24 was cloned into the pLVX-
TetOne-EGFP vector to generate the recombinant pLVX-TetOne-EGFP-ZNF24
vector. HEK-293T cells were then co-transfected with recombinant pLVX-
TetOne-EGFP-ZNF24, pSPAX2, and pMD2.G vectors to prepare the lentivirus
particle. Lentivirus particle infection induced ZNF24 overexpression. Stable
cell clones were selected by treating 2 μg/mL puromycin. The viral titer for
the pLVX-TetOne-EGFP-ZNF24 vector was 1 × 108 TU/mL. Cells were treated
with 1 μg/mL Dox at indicated time to induce ZNF24 overexpression.
Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from BC cell lines using TRIpure lysate (BioTeke,
Beijing, China) and quantiﬁed by NanoDrop One Ultraviolet spectro-
photometer (Thermo Scientiﬁc, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). RNA was then reverse
transcribed using the All-in-One First-Strand SuperMix (Magen Biotechnol-
ogy, Guangzhou, China). Primers for qPCR were generated from General
Biology, Inc (Anhui, China) and presented in Supplementary Table 1. The
qPCR ampliﬁcation was conducted by using the SYBR GREEN (Solarbio) and
PCR MasterMix (Solarbio). The data obtained were analyzed by using
ExicyclerTM 96 ﬂuorescence quantiﬁer (Bioneer Corporation, Daejeon,
Korea). GAPDH served as the internal control.
MTT assay
The 96-well plates were used to seed cells (6 × 103 cells/well) and exposed
them to Dox (1 μg/mL) for 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, or 60 h, separately. MTT solution

Clonogenic growth
BC cells were transferred to Petri dishes at 300 cells per dish. Following
2 weeks incubation in medium supplemented with or without Dox (1 μg/

formation rate was calculated.
EdU assay
EdU incorporation assay (KeyGEN) was used to measure cell prolifera-
tion. 2 × 105 cells were seeded in each well of a 24-well ﬂat-bottom
plate. The cells were incubated with EdU (10 μM) for 2 h, and then ﬁxed
with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After
removing the buffer, the cells were incubated with Click-iT reaction
solution for 30 min in the dark. The nucleus was stained with DAPI for
5 min. A ﬂuorescence microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to
obtain images (×400).
Migration and invasion assay
Cell migration and invasion assay was performed in Transwell chambers
(24-well). Before invasion assay, the chamber was coated with Matrigel
(Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) previously. Transwell chambers
(24-well) without Matrigel coating was used for cell migration detection.
Cell suspension (200 μL) was added into the top chamber of transwell,
whereas 800 μL of FBS-contained medium was added in the bottom
chamber. After the cell migration at 37 °C for 24 h, nonmigrating cells on
the top of membrane were carefully removed by PBS washing. The cells
that have migrated to the lower surface of membrane were ﬁxed with 4%
paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min and stained with 0.5%
crystal violet for 5 min. The number of migrated cells was counted using an
Olympus ﬂuorescence microscopy (×100).
Xenograft mouse model and pulmonary metastasis model
The 6-week-old male nude mice were used to evaluate the effects of
ZNF24 knockdown or overexpression on tumor growth and tumorigenicity.
5637 cells (1 × 107 cells) carrying dox-inducible ZNF24 overexpression,
silencing, or control vectors were injected subcutaneously into nude mice.
Dox diet (drinking water containing 2 mg/mL Dox and 10 mg/mL sucrose)
was given to mice when palpable tumors were observed. The volume of
tumor was monitored every 4 days for 32 days. The bioluminescence
system detected tumor growth in vivo and calculated light intensity.
Finally, mice were sacriﬁced and the tumor tissues were weighted and
photos were collected.
The mice were injected with the above cells (2 × 105 cells) via tail vein
and immediately followed by giving the Dox diet (drinking water
containing 2 mg/mL Dox and 10 mg/mL sucrose) to established pulmonary
metastasis models. Lung metastasis of BC cells was detected after 6 weeks.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
Formalin-ﬁxed xenograft tumors were subjected to parafﬁn embedding

obtained using the microscope (×100, Olympus).
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
Cells were treated with a ﬁnal concentration of 1% formaldehyde for
10 min to cross-link the target protein with the corresponding genomic
DNA. The crosslinking was stopped by adding 1.1 mL glycine solution.
Subsequently, the cells were lysed and subjected to ultrasound treatment
to obtain suitable DNA fragments. DNA fragments were then diluted and
the following antibodies were used for immunoprecipitation: anti-ZNF24
(Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA). Elution buffer was used to elute the
chromatin from the antibody/protein G magnetic beads. The protein-DNA
crosslinks were removed by heating at 65 °C for 4 h and then the DNA
samples were incubated with Proteinase K for 60 min at 45 °C. DNA was
collected by centrifugation for sequencing.

ligated fragments were then ampliﬁed and underwent sequenced. The
sequencing images were generated by the sequencing platform and
transformed into Raw Reads or Raw Data and stored in FASTQ format. The
fastp software was used for Raw Data trimming. After ﬁltering out the
joints and low-quality reads, clean reads were aligned to the human

In vitro SUMOylation assays
The in vitro ZNF24 SUMOylation was measured by using Ni2+-NTA pull
down assay. In brief, after plasmid transfection for 48 h, cells were collected
and part of cells (25%) were lysed by RIPA buffer (Solarbio) for western
blotting. The remaining cells were lysed in His-lysis buffer (3 mL, containing
6 M guanidinium-HCl). Ni2+-NTA-agarose beads (60 μL, Qiagen, Hilden,
Germany) were then added to the lysates and incubated at 4 °C overnight.
The beads were successively washed with washing buffers 1–4 (50 μL each,
containing 8 M urea) for 5 min at room temperature. Then, the beads were
incubated in elution buffer (75 μL) for 20 min at room temperature. The
eluates were analyzed by western blotting.
In vivo SUMOylation assays
The endogenous SUMOylation was detected by Co-IP assay based on
previous studies with minor changes [23, 24]. Cells were lysed with RIPA
buffer containing 20 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM), protease inhibitor
cocktail (Roche, Nutley, NJ, USA), and 5 mM DTT. The lysates were
sonicated and boiled for 10 min. After removal of cell debris by
centrifugation, the target antibody and incubation buffer were added
into the protein lysate overnight at 4 °C. Then, protein A + G beads were
added to the lysate at 4 °C for 4 h. Immunoprecipitants were eluted with
elution buffer and underwent SDS-PAGE.

RESULTS
ZNF24 is downregulated in BC tissues and cells and ZNF24 is
positively correlated with prognosis of BC patients
ZNF24 expressions in BC cells and tissues were ﬁrst detected, and
ZNF24 protein levels in BC cell lines (T24, J82, 5637, TCCSUP, and
HT-1376) were signiﬁcantly decreased compared with human
urothelial cells (SV-HUC-1) (Fig. 1A). The same expression trend of
ZNF24 was also observed in BC cancer tissues (n = 20) compared
to para-cancer tissues (Fig. 1B, C). The relationship between ZNF24
expression (high or low) and clinicopathological characteristics of
BC patients was analyzed. Staining index scores ≥6 were
considered highly expressed, while scores <6 were identiﬁed as
low expression. The representative pictures of ZNF24 high and low
expression were shown in SF. 1A. Meanwhile, the ZNF24 staining
(sections from the same person) in normal bladder tissues was
obtained from The Human Protein Atlas website (SF. 1B). It was
shown that different expression of ZNF24 had no signiﬁcant
impact on age, gender, tumor size, and lymph node metastasis
(Fig. 1D). Patients of BC with stages III-IV preferred to have low
ZNF24 expression (Fig. 1D). Consistently, low expression of ZNF24
in BC tissues was also found on related bioinformatics websites
and BC patients with higher ZNF24 showed better prognosis than
that with lower ZNF24 expression (Fig. 1E).
ZNF24 suppresses cell growth in vitro and in vivo
Dox-inducible expression vectors were used to achieve ZNF24
overexpression or silencing in BC cells (Fig. 2A). ZNF24 expression
was veriﬁed by RT-qPCR (SF. 2A). It was shown that ZNF24
overexpression caused a signiﬁcant decrease in cell viability (SF.
2B). The decreased EdU-positive cells and reduced colony
formation ability were also indicated the disrupted cell prolifera-
tion post-ZNF24 overexpression (Fig. 2B, C). ZNF24 knockdown
enhanced the proliferation ability of BC cells.
The effects of ZNF24 on the tumorigenicity of BC cells in vivo
were explored. After injecting stable ZNF24-overexpressed or
-silenced cells into subcutaneous tissues of nude mice, mice were
fed with Dox-containing water when the tumor was visible.
Bioluminescent imaging of the mouse models showed that
smaller tumors were formed by 5637 cells in the ZNF24
overexpression group than in the Ctrl group (Fig. 3A–C). Tumor
volume was calculated in the animals over time, the data
indicated that tumor growth was signiﬁcantly suppressed by
ZNF24 upregulation (Fig. 3D). The decreased tumor weight also
suggested impaired tumor growth (Fig. 3E). Results of IHC staining
showed that ZNF24 expression in tumor tissues were successfully
induced (Fig. 3F). The detection of Ki67 by IHC staining further
veriﬁed the effects of ZNF24 on tumor growth (Fig. 3F). All in all,
those results suggests that ZNF24 overexpression can slow down
the growth of tumors.
ZNF24 inhibits BC cell metastasis in vitro and in vivo
Metastases are characteristic of most malignancies and respon-
sible for many cancer-related deaths. The effects of ZNF24 on cell
metastasis were further detected. Post-ZNF24 overexpression, the
migration and invasion abilities of T24 and 5637 cells were
weakened (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, stable ZNF24-overexpressed or
silenced cells were injected into nude mice via tail vein, lung
metastasis was evaluated after 6 weeks. Mice with exogenous

Pan-SUMOylation inhibition of ZNF24 promotes protein
degradation of ZNF24 by inhibiting the ubiquitin-proteasome
pathway
SUMOylation is found to inﬂuence the functions of proteins by
regulating their properties such as protein stability. We used a
pan-SUMOylation inhibitor TAK-981 to inhibit ZNF24 SUMOylation
in T24 and 5637 cells, the results showed that for the input, TAK-
981 treatment decreased the amount of SUMO1 and SUMO1-
substrates, which is similar to previous studies [25]. Meanwhile,
ZNF24 SUMOylation was successfully suppressed (Fig. 7G). After
the inhibition of ZNF24 SUMOylation, ZNF24 ubiquitination in
5637 cells were measured, TAK-981 treatment also increased the
ubiquitination of ZNF24 (Fig. 7H). Furthermore, TAK-981 treatment
promoted the degradation of SUMO1 and SUMO1-substrates,
including ZNF24 (SF. 7A and Fig. 7I). Those observations indicate
that SUMOylation stabilizes ZNF24 via depressing ubiquitination.
UBC9 increases ZNF24 SUMOylation
UBC9 is the only E2 conjugating enzyme for SUMOylation. The
expression correlation between ZNF24 and UBC9 at RNA levels in
BC was analyzed using GEPIA website, and a signiﬁcant positive
correlation (R = 0.5) was found between them, indicating the
positive regulatory relationship between ZNF24 and UBC9 (Fig.
8A). Co-IP and IF assay veriﬁed the protein interaction between
ZNF24 and UBC9 (Fig. 8B, C). Flag-tagged UBC9 was transfected
into T24 and 5637 cells to detect the effects of exogenous UBC9
on endogenous ZNF24 SUMOylation (Fig. 8D). It was shown that
UBC9 overexpression increased SUMOylated ZNF24 (Fig. 8D).
ZNF24 is SUMOylated at K27 in a UBC9-dependent manner
The amino acid residue K27 of ZNF24 was predicted to rank the
highest possibility to be SUMOylated using the GPS-SUMO
website (Fig. 8E). The lysine residue K27 is highly evolutionally
conserved in various species (Fig. 8F). We constructed single
mutation plasmid Myc-ZNF24K27R via site-directed mutagenesis
(Fig. 8F), and no difference was occurred between the 3D structure
(SWISS-MODEL, https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) of wild-type and
K27 mutant ZNF24 (Fig. 8G). The His-tagged SUMO1, Flag-tagged
UBC9, and Myc-tagged ZNF24 (WT or K27R) were co-expressed in
HEK293T cells, Ni2+-NTA pull down was used to detect SUMOy-
lated ZNF24, the results showed that K27 mutation decreased the
SUMOylation of ZNF24 in the absence and presence of UBC9 (Fig.
8H). The Myc-tagged ZNF24 (WT or K27R) was respectively co-
transfected with HA-tagged ubiquitin and Flag-tagged UBC9 into
HEK293T cells to analyze the relations between SUMOylation at
K27 and ubiquitination of ZNF24 (Fig. 8I). The ubiquitination of
exogenous ZNF24 was decreased after the introduction of
exogenous UBC9 but K27 mutation increased ZNF24 ubiquitina-
tion (Fig. 8I). The Myc-tagged ZNF24 (WT or K27R) was co-
transfected with Flag-tagged UBC9 into 5637 cells, the Myc/ZNF24
and Flag/UBC9 protein levels in 5637 cells were ﬁrst detected (SF.
6A). ZNF24 protein and Flag-tagged UBC9 levels in 5637 cells were
then measured at each time point after CHX addition (Fig. 8J and
SF. 7B). The results showed that UBC9 induction blocked ZNF24
degradation
in
5637
cells
but
K27
mutation
signiﬁcantly
weakened this blockade (Fig. 8J). The protein stability of Flag-
tagged UBC9 did not change signiﬁcantly among all groups (SF.
7B). Those observations indicate that K27 may be the key site
affecting the ubiquitination and SUMOylation of ZNF24.

protein degradation rates of exogenous CUL3 and ubi did not
change in cells with or without UBC9 induction (SF. 7F).
DISCUSSION
Posttranslational modiﬁcation provides a key functional regulatory
link
in
tumorigenesis,
cancer
progression,
and
metastasis.
Intracellular signaling proteins such as p53 and signal transducer
and activator of transcription 1 are known to be SUMOylated to
regulate tumorigenesis [26]. However, the regulatory mechanism
of SUMOylation and the interaction between SUMOylated proteins
and intracellular signaling proteins during tumor progression and
metastasis are still unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that
transcription factor ZNF24 is a new SUMOylation target protein,
and the SUMOylation of ZNF24 positively regulates its stability and
inhibits the growth and metastasis of BC. ZNF24 is SUMOylated at
K27 site by UBC9 with SUMO1 modiﬁcation. CUL3, an E3 ubiquitin
ligase, is shown to mediate the degradation of ZNF24. ZNF24
SUMOylation inhibits CUL3-mediated proteasomal degradation of
ZNF24. The downstream mechanisms of ZNF24 in BC develop-
ment are identiﬁed through high-throughput omics analysis.
Li et al. performed a transcriptional analysis of ZNF24 in
293 T cells, revealing the pleiotropic effects of ZNF24, such as
kinase activity, transcription, angiogenesis, and DNA damage [27].
This pleiotropy is also seen in cancer progression, with ZNF24
acting as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer but not in
hepatocellular carcinoma [14, 28]. The decreased ZNF24 expres-
sion in BC cells and tissues, poor prognosis in patients with low
expression of ZNF24, and enhanced malignant phenotypes in
ZNF24-decreased cells demonstrate the tumor suppressive effect
of ZNF24 in BC progression. ZNF24 contains four Kruppel-like
C2H2 zinc ﬁnger domains at the C-terminal, which serve as DNA-
binding domains [13, 29]. The domains speciﬁcally bind to
chromosomal loci, thereby activating or inhibiting target gene
transcription [21, 30]. The results of ChIP-seq and mRNA-seq were
analyzed comprehensively, 123 genes that might be regulated by
ZNF24 transcription. The functional enrichment of these genes is
multifunctional, including cell growth and metastasis, such as
growth factor binding, apoptosis, focal adhesion, etc, which is
veriﬁed in the present study. Previous reports also showed the

Although
ZNF24
can
be
SUMOylated
by
SUMO1/2,
the
SUMOylation is mainly carried out by SUMO1, and the SUMOyla-
tion improves the stability of ZNF24 by inhibiting the ubiquitina-
tion of ZNF24. The SUMOylation of ZNF24 in BC cells and tissues
was reduced, which may be the reason for the decreased
expression of ZNF24 in BC cells and tissues. Through molecular
docking, it was found that the protein interaction domain
between SUMO1 and ZNF24 contained a K27 site, which is also
a SUMOylation modiﬁcation site of ZNF24. This is a reliable basis
for SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation of ZNF24. SUMOylation not
only affects protein stability but also protein localization and
interaction [36, 37]. This study mainly focuses on the changes of
protein stability of ZNF24, and whether SUMOylated ZNF24
change protein localization and interaction will be explored in
future
experiments.
After
K27
mutation,
the
SUMOylation
modiﬁcation
of
ZNF24
decreased
while
the
ubiquitination
modiﬁcation increased, indicating that although there are several
potential SUMO modiﬁcation sites on ZNF24, K27 may be an
important site for regulating the stability of ZNF24 protein. The
appearance of this competitive-like modiﬁcation may be due to
K27 site not only being a SUMOylation modiﬁcation site but also a
ubiquitination modiﬁcation site, or the SUMOylation of ZNF24
may affect the binding of ubiquitin protease to ZNF24. We
attempted to identify factors that affect the stability of ZNF24 and
focused on CUL3. Our results indicate that the CUL3-mediate
ZNF24 degradation is weakened after induction of SUMOylation
modiﬁcation. Meanwhile, through molecular docking, the binding
of CUL3 to ZNF24 was close to the K27 site and did not contain
the K27 site, which rules out the possibility of placeholder
modiﬁcation. Thus, we speculate that the SUMOylation of ZNF24
may affect the protein interaction between CUL3 and ZNF24,
which needs to be conﬁrmed by further experiments. In this study,
we have explained the importance of K27 site for maintaining the
stability of ZNF24 protein from the molecular level, but whether
this site affects the malignant behaviors of cancer cells is
unknown, which is what our next experiment needs to do.
Overall,
our
observations
provide
novel
insights
into
the
regulatory cross-talk between SUMOylation and ubiquitination of
ZNF24 in BC progression.
