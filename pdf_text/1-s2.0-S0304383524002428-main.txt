A B S T R A C T  
Branched-chain amino acid transferase 1 (BCAT1) is highly expressed in multiple cancers and is associated with 
poor prognosis, particularly in glioblastoma (GBM). However, the post-translational modification (PTM) 
mechanism of BCAT1 is unknown. Here, we investigated the cross-talk mechanisms between phosphorylation 
and ubiquitination modifications in regulating BCAT1 activity and stability. We found that BCAT1 is phos­
phorylated by branched chain ketoacid dehydrogenase kinase (BCKDK) at S5, S9, and T312, which increases its 
catalytic and antioxidant activity and stability. STUB1 (STIP1 homology U-box-containing protein 1), the first we 
found and reported E3 ubiquitin ligase of BCAT1, can also be phosphorylated by BCKDK at the S19 site, which 
disrupts the interaction with BCAT1 and inhibits its degradation. In addition, we demonstrate through in vivo and 
in vitro experiments that BCAT1 phosphorylation inhibiting its ubiquitination at multiple sites is associated with 
GBM proliferation and that inhibition of the BCKDK-BCAT1 axis enhances the sensitivity to temozolomide (TMZ). 
Overall, we identified novel mechanisms for the regulation of BCAT1 modification and elucidated the importance 
of the BCKDK-STUB1-BCAT1 axis in GBM progression.   
1. Introduction 
Branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism regulates tumor cell 
reprogramming mediating the progression of multiple cancers [1–3]. 
Branched-chain amino acid transaminase (BCAT) mediates the catabo­
lism of BCAAs to produce glutamate, which is further synthesized into 
glutamine, a critical nutrient for glioblastoma (GBM) cell growth [4,5]. 
BCAT has two isoforms, BCAT1 and BCAT2, located in the cytoplasm 
and mitochondria, respectively. BCAT2 is widely expressed [6], whereas 
BCAT1 is mostly distributed in the nervous system, and an increasing 
number of studies have shown that BCAT1 is highly expressed in many 
tumors, particularly GBM, and is associated with cancer progression and 
poor prognosis [7–10]. It has been shown that BCAT1 is clearly 
expressed in IDH1(Isocitrate Dehydrogenase 1)WT, but not in the ma­
jority of IDH1Mut gliomas. As there is abundant methylation within the 
promoter region of BCAT1 in IDH1Mut gliomas, whereas suppression of 
BCAT1 blocks glutamate synthesis and leads to reduced aggressiveness 
in glioma [8]. Further studies showed that BCAT1 could maintain the 
poorly differentiated characteristics of glioblastoma cells and contrib­
utes to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which was 
expected to be a marker of tumour progression and prognosis [11]. 
Existing BCAT1-related regulatory mechanisms mainly focus on the 
transcriptional level, including the upstream transcription factors c-Myc

[12], SMAD5 [13], MSI2 [14], PBX3 [15], and some miRNAs [16] and 
lncRNAs [17]. And targeted inhibition of FLT3 also inhibits BCAT1 
expression [18]. Transcriptional regulation greatly influences the 
expression of BCAT1, but the mechanisms of BCAT1 itself at the 
post-translational modification (PTM) level have not yet been 
investigated. 
Protein PTMs are essential for the maintenance of normal cellular 
activities [19,20]. Phosphorylation and ubiquitination are the most 
common types of PTMs that can affect protein biological function by 
changing structures, protein interactions, stability or location, and their 
aberrant regulation has been associated with the development of certain 
diseases and cancers [21–23]. Branched-chain α-keto acid dehydroge­
nase kinase (BCKDK), a key enzyme in BCAA catabolism, is expressed in 
a variety of tumors and may serve as an emerging serine/threonine (S/T) 
kinase target in cancer [24–29]. Our recent study found that BCKDK is 
highly expressed in GBM and promotes the proliferation of tumor cells 
(unpublished), but whether there is a correlation between BCAT1 and 
BCKDK remains unknown. STIP1 homology and U-box-containing pro­
tein 1 (STUB1) is a co-chaperone of heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) and 
an E3 ligase that has been demonstrated to be a therapeutic target for a 
variety of neurological disorders and plays an important role in aging, 
autophagy, and cancer progression [30,31]. The expression of STUB1 in 
GBM has a dual function, with high expression in U87 and U251 cells 
promoting gliomagenesis [32], while also inhibiting the progression of 
GBM by targeting the degradation of EGFR and c-Myc [33,34]. There­
fore, exploration of other substrates of STUB1 is necessary to fully un­
derstand its role in GBM. 
Here, we first identified that BCKDK is a direct upstream kinase of 
BCAT1 that interacts with and phosphorylates BCAT1 at S5/S9/T312 to 
increase its catalytic and antioxidant activity and stability. We also 
confirmed that STUB1 is a novel E3 ligase of BCAT1 and is phosphory­
lated by BCKDK. BCKDK-mediated phosphorylation of BCAT1 inhibits 
its ubiquitination degradation by STUB1 and promotes the growth of 
GBM. In addition, inhibition of the BCKDK-BCAT1 axis significantly 
enhanced the sensitivity to TMZ and inhibited GBM progression in vitro 
and in vivo. Altogether, this study emphasizes the importance of the 
novel BCKDK-STUB1-BCAT1 signaling axis in GBM treatment.

analyzed and presented using FlowJo V10 and GraphPad Prism 9.

When the cells grew to approximately 80 % confluency, the medium 
was changed to serum-free medium, and the cells were cultured for 12 h.

well plates. Each well of a 200 μL reaction system contained 100 mM 
Tris (pH 7.4), 50 μM pyridoxal phosphate (Shanghai Yuanye), 5 mM

(Shanghai Yuanye), 100 μg GLDH1 (glutamate dehydrogenase 1)

the BCAT1 sample, and the rate of NADH formation was immediately 
subjected to kinetic, spectrophotometric quantification of A340 nm to 
assess BCAT1 enzyme activity. Readings were acquired after incubation 
at 37 ◦C for 1 h.

3. Results 
3.1. BCKDK up-regulates the protein expression of BCAT1 in GBM 
Abnormalities in BCAA metabolism are strongly associated with 
GBM growth [36,37]. The analysis revealed a correlation between the 
expression of two key molecules of BCAA metabolism (BCKDK & 
BCAT1) in the TCGA-GBM database (Fig. 1A). For further confirmation, 
genes associated with the regulation of BCAA metabolism, cell prolif­
eration, and tumor grading in the three GBM databases (TCGA, CGGA, 
and IVY) were analyzed and classified into two clusters of high and low 
expression based on the ssGSEA score (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Subse­
quently, we performed a Venn analysis of these clusters (Supplementary 
Figs. 1B–D) and obtained 14 common genes (Fig. 1B). Among these 
genes, BCAT1 and BCKDK, key BCAA catabolism enzymes, changed in 
the same trend (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1E) and were highly 
expressed in GBM compared to LGG (Fig. 1D) and showed a positive 
correlation (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1F). We also detected the 
expression of BCAT1 and BCKDK in 6 glioma cells by WB and found that 
the protein levels of both were consistent (Fig. 1F). Fig. 1G shows that 
BCAT1 and BCKDK were colocalized in GBM cells. To further clarify the 
correlation between BCKDK and BCAT1, we observed that the protein 
but not the mRNA level of BCAT1 was significantly reduced in 
sgBCKDK-or BT2 (BCKDK inhibitor)-treated cells (Fig. 1H, Supplemen­
tary Figs. 1G and H) and increased in BCKDK-overexpressing cells 
(Fig. 1I). These results indicated that BCKDK can upregulate the 
expression of BCAT1 at the level of protein rather than transcript, but 
the detailed mechanism still has to be clarified. 
3.2. BCAT1 phosphorylation by BCKDK promotes GBM growth by 
increasing its enzymatic and antioxidant activities 
BCKDK, as a S/T protein kinase, can influence the biological function 
of substrates through phosphorylation, such as MEK(27), BCKDHA [38], 
ACL [39], and PDC [40]. Here, we tested a hypothesis that BCKDK could 
directly phosphorylate BCAT1 to improve its function. First, the results 
from IP and pull-down assays revealed that BCKDK could bind with 
BCAT1 (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Figs. 2A and B) in vitro. Next, in vitro 
IP-kinase assay results showed that BCKDK directly phosphorylated 
BCAT1 (Fig. 2B). S5, S9, and T312 on BCAT1 were identified as specific 
phosphorylation sites of BCKDK by mass spectrometry (MS) (Fig. 2C), 
and these sites have some genetic conservation (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

has been reported. Based on KEGG enrichment analysis of the BCAT1 
gene in the TCGA database, BCAT1 was found to be associated with the 
ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis pathway (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Next, 
we confirmed that BCAT1 can be ubiquitinated and exists in K48- and 
K63-linked ubiquitination forms in HEK293T cells by cotransfecting the 
HA-Ub plasmid (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the ubiquitina­
tion level of BCAT1 was detected by IP with an anti-Myc antibody by 
cotransfecting HA-Ub, BCKDK-Flag, and Myc-BCAT1 into HEK293T cells 
for 48 h. Fig. 3B shows that the ubiquitination level of BCAT1 in the 
cotransfected BCKDK group was the lowest, which confirmed that 
BCKDK could inhibit the ubiquitination of BCAT1. The same results were 
observed in BCAT1-3A- and 3D-overexpressing cells. BCAT1-3A t1/2 was 
shorter than that of BCAT1-WT and BCAT1-3D (Fig. 3C–E), and the 
ubiquitination level of BCAT1-3A was more pronounced (Fig. 3D–F). 
Together, 
these 
results 
demonstrated 
that 
BCKDK-mediated 
phosphorylation of BCAT1 at the S5, S9, and T312 sites protect it from 
ubiquitination degradation, thereby enhancing its stability. 
3.4. STUB1 is the E3 ubiquitin ligase of BCAT1 
To identify the ubiquitin ligase (E3) that mediates ubiquitination of 
BCAT1, BCAT1-His complexes were purified by IP from HEK293T cell 
lysates exogenously transfected with BCAT1-His and analyzed by MS. 
According to proteins identified from the BCAT1-His IP group only or 
≥3-fold (ratio) compared with the control group, we identified four E3s 
that were associated with BCAT1 from all 3 biological replicates 
(Fig. 4A). Among them, the expression of STUB1 was negatively corre­
lated with BCAT1 in all grades of gliomas based on the CGGA database 
(Supplementary Fig. 4A). To confirm whether STUB1 is an E3 ligase of 
BCAT1, we observed that STUB1 could interact with BCAT1 by endog­
enous/exogenous IP and pull-down experiments (Fig. 4B and C, Sup­
plementary Figs. 4B and C), and compared to BCAT1-WT and 3D, STUB1 
had the strongest binding capacity to BCAT1-3A (Supplementary 
Fig. 4D). Through protein interaction domain mapping, we designed and 
constructed a truncated STUB1 prokaryotic expression vector and found 
that BCAT1 physically interacted with STUB1 through its N-terminal 
TPR domain by pull-down experiments (Fig. 4D). Molecular docking 
experiments also demonstrated the physical binding of the TPR struc­
tural domain to BCAT1 (Fig. 4E). Then, we also found that the protea­
some inhibitor MG132 could restore BCAT1 expression in STUB1- 
overexpressing cells, but the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) 
could not (Fig. 4F), which suggested that STUB1 facilitates 
ubiquitination-mediated degradation of BCAT1 via a proteasomal 
pathway. We further observed that the BCAT1 concentration was 
attenuated in a dose-dependent manner in STUB1-overexpressing 
HEK293T cells (Fig. 4G) and had a long t1/2 of 3–9 h in shSTUB1 
U118MG cells (Fig. 4H). We further confirmed that STUB1 primarily 
increased K48-linked ubiquitination of BCAT1 rather than the K63- 
linked form (Fig. 4I) and that mutant STUB1-H260Q (no E3 catalytic 
activity) reduced BCAT1 K48-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 4J). Together, 
these data suggested that STUB1 is E3 ligase of BCAT1 and mediated 
K48-linked ubiquitination degradation of BCAT1 through TPR domain 
binding.

3.6. BCKDK-mediated phosphorylation of STUB1 at S19 disrupts the 
interaction with BCAT1 
It was shown that both S/T protein kinases PKG (protein kinase G) 
and CDK5 (cyclin-dependent kinase 5) phosphorylate STUB1 at S19 and 
thus influence the development of cardiac and neuronal systemic dis­
eases [46,47]. Therefore, we wanted to determine whether BCKDK 
could phosphorylate STUB1 while simultaneously phosphorylating 
BCAT1. According to two independent IP-MS and SILAC + IP-MS ex­
periments, phosphorylated peptides of STUB1 at S19 were detected in 
IP-BCKDK (Fig. 6A, Supplemental Table 6) and SILAC samples 
(Fig. 6B–Supplemental Table 6). The results indicated that BCKDK could 
directly phosphorylate STUB1 at S19. Subsequently, IF and IP confirmed 
that BCKDK and STUB1 were colocalized and interacted with each other 
in cells (Fig. 6C–E and Supplementary Figs. 6A–C). The expression of 
BCKDK was positively correlated with that of STUB1 (Supplementary 
Figs. 6D–F). Further IP kinase assays showed that phosphorylated 
STUB1 levels increased in the WT group and decreased in the S19A 
group (Fig. 6F). These data validated that BCKDK was able to bind with 
and phosphorylate STUB1 at S19. 
Previous studies have shown that STUB1 phosphorylation at S19 
does not affect its E3 ligase activity but promotes dissociation from its 
substrate [47]. We also obtained the same results by IP experiments in 
which EGF-stimulated BCKDK did not decrease its level of 
autoubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. 6G) but suppressed STUB1 
binding with BCAT1, especially with BCAT1-3A (Fig. 6G). Moreover, the 
capacity of the S19A mutant STUB1 to interact with BCAT1 also 
decreased when BCKDK was activated by EGF (Fig. 6H). Taken together, 
BCKDK is a novel kinase of STUB1 and inhibits the interaction between 
STUB1 and BCAT1 by both phosphorylation modifications. 
3.7. Inhibition of BCKDK and BCAT1 enhances TMZ therapeutic 
sensitivity in GBM 
BCKDK was found to be a candidate marker for TMZ resistance in 
GBM treatment [48]. To assess the therapeutic effect of inhibiting the 
BCKDK-STUB1-BCAT1 signaling axis in combination with TMZ for GBM, 
U118MG or GWH04 cells treated with BT2, GABA (BCAT1 inhibitor), 
and TMZ alone or in combination were observed by colony formation, 
soft agar and EdU assays. The results showed that the triple combination 
significantly inhibited GBM cell proliferation (Fig. 7A–C, Supplementary 
Fig. 7A) and induced apoptosis (Fig. 7D, Supplementary Figs. 7B and C) 
compared with the other groups. We further evaluated the drug com­
bination therapeutic effects of GBM in vivo through an intracranial 
model. The TMZ, BT2 and GABA combination significantly inhibited the 
growth of tumors compared with either drug alone (Fig. 7F–G, Supple­
mentary Fig. 7D) and improved the prognosis of mice, which had longer 
survival (Fig. 7H). Moreover, these drugs did not lead to hepatic or renal 
impairment damage in mice (Supplementary Fig. 7E). These results 
suggested that targeting the BCKDK-BCAT1 axis is an effective thera­
peutic strategy to ameliorate the TMZ response in GBM. 
4. Discussion 
Increasing findings have demonstrated that altered BCAA meta­
bolism affects tumor growth and progression, and that BCAAs affect 
tumor growth differently depending on the type of tumor tissue (even 
with the same tumor driver gene) [3,49]. During tumor growth, BCA 
provides nitrogen for the de novo synthesis of nucleotides and amino 
acids; its decomposition provides carbon for the synthesis of other 
molecules and provides energy to cells by promoting TCA cycle and 
oxidative phosphorylation; secondly, BCA metabolism can affect the 
levels of metabolites-derived cofactors such as α -ketoglutaric acid (α 
-KG) and glutamate (Glu), thus activating signaling pathways to reshape 
epigenetic inheritance and helping cancer cells survive [1]. Therefore, 
an in-depth understanding of the role of BCAAs and their catabolism in 
cancer pathogenesis and exploring the abnormal expression of molecu­
lar expression during BCAA metabolism and the mechanism of action 
can contribute to the study of cancer therapeutic strategies and 
interventions.

Metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells is common in all malig­
nancies, including GBM [50]. Among them, the marked up-regulation of 
BCAA metabolism in GBM provides a reliable basis for clinical staging, 
differential diagnosis, and treatment [51,52]. BCAT1 mediates BCAA 
catabolism and is specifically highly expressed in GBM, thereby pro­
moting cancer progression [8,53]. However, the existing modulation of 
BCAT1 is mainly at the transcriptional level, and the mechanism of 
modification regulation of BCAT1 expression has not been reported. In 
this study, we identified two novel modifications, BCAT1 phosphoryla­
tion and ubiquitination, and clarified the cross-talk mechanism regu­
lating its activity and stability. First, we found that S5, S9, and T312 of 
BCAT1 were phosphorylated by BCKDK by an in vitro kinase assay to 
increase its enzymatic activity, antioxidant activity and stability. The E3 
ubiquitin ligase STUB1 of BCAT1 was further screened by IP-MS, and its 
ubiquitination site was clarified. We also found that BCKDK was able to 
phosphorylate STUB1 to disrupt its binding to the substrate BCAT1, 
thereby increasing BCAT1 stability and promoting GBM progression. In 
conclusion, we identified the importance of the BCKDK-STUB1-BCAT1 
axis, highlighting a novel mechanism of BCAT1 regulation through the 
cross-talk of multiple modifications during GBM development. 
Increasing evidence suggests that elevated activity or high expres­
sion of BCAT1 triggered by multiple mechanisms can promote the pro­
gression of a variety of tumors [54]. The BCAT1E61A mutation was 
found to be present in gastric cancer tissue, which can promote BCAT1 
activity and is associated with cancer progression [55]. On the other 
hand, transcription factors such as c-Myc [12] and MSI2 [14] can 
directly bind BCAT1 and promote its transcript levels to facilitate 
nasopharyngeal cancer and myeloid leukemia progression. Additionally, 
miRNAs (such as miR-98–5p [16], miR-124–3p [56], miR-203 [17], 
miR-218 [57], miR-506 [58]) can be directly targeted to inhibit BCAT1 
mRNA levels in esophageal cancer, gastric cancer, hepatocellular cancer 
and glioma. The histone lysine methyltransferase DOT1L up-regulates 
the expression of BCAT1 through the conversion of histone H3 
methylation in breast cancer cells [59]. All of these mechanisms are 
regulated at the level of BCAT1 transcription. Here, our study first re­
veals the new regulatory mechanisms of BCAT1 at the level of PTMs. 
First, we found that BCAT1 can be phosphorylated at S5, S9, and T312 
and identified BCKDK as a direct upstream kinase of BCAT1. BCAT1 
phosphorylation could increase its catalytic and antioxidant activity and 
stability. Second, we demonstrated that BCAT1 could be ubiquitinated 
at multiple K sites by at least two linked forms, especially K2, K19, and 
K305 adjacent to S5, S9, and T312, and STUB1 was one of the E3 ligases 
that mediated BCAT1 K48-linked ubiquitination. Either phosphorylation 
or ubiquitination of BCAT1 affected GBM growth and therapeutic 
sensitivity. In addition, we found different forms of ubiquitination (e.g., 
ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway or autophagy‒lysosome pathway) at 
different K sites of BCAT1, which implies that there may be other forms 
of modification mechanisms that can influence BCAT1 function. 
Phosphorylation and ubiquitination are the most common PTMs of 
proteins in eukaryotes. It has been shown that cross-talk between 
phosphorylation and ubiquitination seriously impacts cell biological 
behavior and is considered a perfect disease therapeutic strategy. ERK- 
mediated phosphorylation of the BAG3 S377 site promotes its 
FBXO22-ubiquitinated 
degradation 
to 
enhance 
tumor 
growth, 
suggesting that inhibition of BAG3 phosphorylation or FBXO22 
expression may contribute to tumor therapy [42]. AMPK-dependent 
phosphorylation at T222 ablates insulin-induced gene (Insig) interac­
tion with E3 ubiquitin ligase gp78 and represses its ubiquitination and 
degradation; therefore, AMPK can be activated by drugs such as met­
formin and A769662 to stabilize Insig to reduce hepatocyte lipid accu­
mulation [60]. These phenomena are consistent with our finding that 
BCKDK-dependent phosphorylation ablates the interaction of BCAT1 
with STUB1 and represses its ubiquitination and degradation. In addi­
tion, we unexpectedly found that BCKDK-mediated phosphorylation at 
S19 also ablates STUB1 interference with BCAT1 and increases the sta­
bility of BCAT1. Augmentation of BCAT1 stability and activity leads to 
GBM growth. BCAT1 and STUB1 were uncovered to be two novel ef­
fectors of BCKDK in addition to BCKDHA [38], MEK [27], ACL [39], and 
PDC [40]. These findings expand the diversity and scope of BCKDK 
regulatory mechanisms and further elucidate the important role of the 
BCKDK-STUB1-BCAT1 axis in GBM development and its potential as a 
targeted therapy. 
Protease-targeted chimeras (PROTACs) are a new technology that 
inhibits protein function by hijacking ubiquitin E3 ligase for protein 
degradation. In recent years, PROTACs have been shown to achieve 
clinical therapeutic effects through a ligand for recruiting E3 ligase, a 
linker, and another ligand to bind to the target protein for degradation 
[61]. Many small-molecule PROTACs, such as CRBN [62], VHL [63], 
IAPs [64], MDM2 [65], DCAF15 [66] and RNF114-based [67] PRO­
TACs, have been explored not only in cancer diseases but also in other 
diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, immune diseases, neurode­
generative diseases, and viral infections. We demonstrated that STUB1 is 
the E3 ubiquitin ligase of BCAT1, and thus, the development of a set of 
BCAT1 PROTACs holds great promise for cancer prevention and 
treatment. 
Limitations of the study 
Although our study demonstrated the phosphorylation and ubiq­
uitination sites and corresponding biological functions of BCAT1, we did 
not successfully prepare anti-BCAT1-specific antibodies recognized 
corresponding to the ubiquitination and phosphorylation sites used for 
cellular research and clinical diagnosis. The mechanism by which 
BCKDK positively regulates STUB1 expression needs to be further clar­
ified (Supplementary Figs. 6D–F), and whether STUB1 can in turn 
ubiquitinate BCKDK needs to be demonstrated in more experiments. 
Furthermore, our studies were observed only in GBM, and the broader 
implications of this mechanism need to be further explored in other 
cancer types and for diseases. Finally, we affirm the efficacy of targeting 
the BCKDK-BCAT1 axis in the treatment of GBM in mice, but more in vivo 
experiments are needed to determine the clinical safety of this 
combination. 
In summary, we discovered a novel regulatory mechanism of BCAT1 
by cross-talk between phosphorylation and ubiquitination and provide a 
promising therapeutic intervention for GBM (see Graphical Abstract, by 
Figdraw).
