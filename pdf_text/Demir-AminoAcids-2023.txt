In silico analysis of the possible crosstalk between O‑linked 
β‑GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation sites of Disabled 1 adaptor 
protein in vertebrates
Ramiz Demir1,2   · Remziye Deveci1 
Received: 13 October 2022 / Accepted: 10 April 2023 / Published online: 17 April 2023

Abstract
Disabled 1 (Dab1) is an adaptor protein with essential functions regulated by reelin signaling and affects many biological 
processes in the nervous system, including cell motility, adhesion, cortical development, maturation, and synaptic plastic-
ity. Posttranslational modifications directly guide the fates of cytoplasmic proteins to complete their functions correctly. 
Reciprocal crosstalk between O-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation is a dynamic modification in cytoplasmic proteins. 
It modulates the functions of the proteins by regulating their interactions with other molecules in response to the continu-
ously changeable cell microenvironment. Although Dab1 contains conserved recognition sites for phosphorylation in their 
N-terminal protein interaction domain, the O-β-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation sites of human Dab1 sequence, their 
reciprocal crosstalk, and potential kinases catalyzing the phosphorylation remain unknown. In this study, we determined 
potential thirty-seven O-β-GlcNAcylation and sixty-seven phosphorylation sites. Conserved twenty-one residues of these 
glycosylated sites were also phosphorylated with various kinases, including ATM, CKI, DNAPK, GSK3, PKC, PKG, RSK, 
cdc2, cdk5, and p38MAPK. In addition, we analyzed these conserved sites at our constructed two- and three-dimensional 
structures of human Dab1 protein. Dab1 protein models were frequently composed of coil structures as well as α-helix and 
β-strands. Many of these conserved crosstalk sites between O-β-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation were localized at the coil 
region of the protein model. These findings may guide biochemical, genetic, and glyco-biology based on further experiments 
about the Dab1 signaling process. Understanding these modifications might change the point of view of the Dab1 signaling 
process and treatment for pathological conditions in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease.

Introduction
Disabled 1 (Dab1) is an adaptor protein characterized by 
a N-terminal protein interaction/phosphotyrosine-binding 
(PI/PTB) domain that interacts with proteins containing 
Src homology-2 (SH2) domain of Src family non-receptor 
tyrosine kinases (Gao et al. 2012; Katyal et al. 2007). It 
also contains two main subdomains consisting of an inter-
nal tyrosine-rich and a C-terminal serine/threonine-rich 
regions (Keshvara et al. 2001). In the tyrosine-rich region, 
Dab1 possesses two conserved recognition sites ­(Y185 and 
­Y198) for Src family kinase and two conserved recogni-
tion sites ­(Y220 and ­Y232) for Abı/Crk (Zhou et al. 1993). 
The tyrosine-phosphorylated Dab1 recruits different SH2 
domain-containing proteins, including p85 regulatory 
subunit of phosphatidylinositide-3-kinase (PI3K), cellu-
lar adaptors CrkL, Crk, Nckβ, and suppressor of cytokine 
signaling (SOCK) (Ballif et al. 2004; Chen et al. 2004; 
Huang et al. 2004; Pramatarova et al. 2003). In these inter-
actions, the distinct tyrosine residues are responsible for 
SH2 domain interactions. For example, ­Y220 and ­Y232 are 
required for the recruitment of Crk, CrkL, and Nckβ adap-
tors, whereas ­Y185 and ­Y198 are also required for PI3K and 
SOCS. The tyrosine-phosphorylated Dab1 also interacts 
with the microtubule-associated protein-1 (Lis1) in an SH2 
domain-independent manner (Assadi et al. 2003).
Dab1–ligand interactions trigger distinct downstream 
signaling cascades to ensure neuronal positioning in Ree-
lin signaling (Gao and Godbout 2013). The Reelin–Dab1 
signaling is a well-established pathway in which Dab1 
interacts with cytoplasmic tail of LDL receptor family 
members, very-low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR) 
and apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (ApoER2) (Benhayon 
et al. 2003; Hiesberger et al. 1999). Abnormal Reelin 
expression is also strictly related with various neuropsy-
chiatric disorders, including autism, schizophrenia, bipolar 
disorder, major depression, Alzheimer’s disease, and lis-
sencephaly (Folsom and Fatemi 2013). Dab1 has func-
tioned as a scaffold protein for many other cytoplasmic 
proteins, such as Crk family adaptors and NckB (Grb4), 
which function in cell motility and adhesion (Ballif et al. 
2004; Huang et al. 2004). In contrast to neuronal develop-
ment, Dab1 expression was also known in non-neuronal 
tissues, including cancer, but the function of Reelin–Dab1 
signaling pathway outside of the nervous system has not 
been studied in detail.
Many proteins are modified post-translationally to 
moderate their structure, activity, solubility, antigenic-
ity, subcellular localization, and overall functions (Uhlén 
et al. 2015). To date, a variety of post-translational modi-
fications (PTMs) have been reported, the phosphorylation 
and O-β-GlcNAcylation are known as dynamic PTMs and 
target identical amino acids in protein. This crosstalk can 
occur by competition for the same sites or influencing each 
other proximity/distal sites on protein sequence (van der 
Laarse et al. 2018). Phosphorylation is the most studied 
PTM that is characterized by covalently linked phosphoryl 
group to the hydroxyl groups of serine, threonine, and 
tyrosine residues in proteins (Nishi et al. 2014). The spe-
cific enzymes called kinases catalyze the process, which 
regulates protein function as their activation or deactiva-
tion by affecting conformation and localization of the pro-
teins (Cohen 2000). Another prevalent PTM is the O-β-
GlcNAcylation, a unique glycosylation type characterized 
by transferring a single sugar moiety called O-linked 
N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) to the hydroxyl groups 
of serine and threonine residues of proteins (Gao et al. 
2001). The O-GlcNAc transferases (OGTs) catalyze the 
O-β-GlcNAcylation process. As an analog to phosphoryla-
tion, the O-β-GlcNAcylation plays an essential role in cell 
signaling and gene regulation (Tarbet et al. 2018). There 
are also ample examples reported of phosphorylation/O-
GlcNAcylation crosstalk affecting signaling molecules 
(Hardivillé and Hart 2014; Yang and Qian 2017; Zachara 
and Hart 2004).
Pathologically, deregulated reciprocal crosstalk of O-β-
GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation has been reported to be 
associated with various disorders, including cardiovascular 
diseases, diabetes, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases 
(Hart et al. 2011; Nie and Yi 2019). Both modifications can 
rapidly be attached and removed from the same protein in 
response to different environmental stimuli such as glucose 
deprivation (Leney et al. 2017). Hence, they serve primar-
ily to modulate various signaling processes in transcription, 
translation, metabolism, and apoptosis (Hart et al. 2011). 
Various neuronal (~ 40%) and synaptosome (~ 19%) proteins 
are O-β-GlcNAcylated as well as phosphorylation in many 
neurodegenerative diseases, including Parkinson’s disease, 
Huntington’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease (Wani et al. 
2017). In addition, many compounds/probes have been used 
to modulate O-β-GlcNAcylation in neurodegenerative and 
cardiovascular diseases (Bolanle et al. 2021). In this regard, 
the development of highly specific inhibitors that modulate 
O-β-GlcNAcylation would lead to the discovery of potential 
new therapeutic approaches that serve as a drug (Park et al. 
2020). Therefore, understanding the crosstalk between O-β-
GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation in the physiopathologi-
cal processes would help uncover novel therapeutic interven-
tion avenues (Zhu and Hart 2021).
Although the Dab1 contains many phosphorylation sites 
that are significant for its interactions with cytoplasmic and 
cell membrane proteins, primarily LDL family receptors and 
reelin–Dab1 axis, the whole phosphorylation sites of Dab1 
remain unknown in detail. The sequential modification of 
proteins through phosphorylation and O-β-GlcNAcylation is

Materials and methods
Retrieval of sequences
To predict potential O-linked β-GlcNAcylation and phos-
phorylation sites of Dab1 adaptor protein, the amino acid 
sequences were retrieved from the databases of National 
Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and Uni-
versal Protein Resource (UniProt) (Table  1). These 
sequences included Homo sapiens (AAH67445.1), Felis 
catus (XP_011283272.1), Sus scrofa (XP_020948793.1), 
Bos taurus (XP_010825298.1), Bos mutus (ELR50858.1), 
Mus musculus (P97318), Macaca fascicularis (Q9BGX5), 
Equus caballus (NP_001229479.1), Rattus norvegicus 
(Q8CJH2), Gallus gallus (NP_989569.1), Cathartes aura 
(KFP50290.1).
Multiple sequence alignment
As a multiple sequence alignment tool, Clustal Omega 
was used to perform multiple alignments for selected 
FASTA sequences of Dab1 adaptor proteins (Madeira et al. 
2022). The phylogenetic tree data that describe the close-
ness among these multiple sequences were obtained from 
the server. The multiple sequence alignment and phylo-
genetic relationship were visualized by phylogenetic tree 
(Newick) viewer of the environment for tree exploration 
(ETE) toolkit, a computational framework that simplifies 
the reconstruction, analysis, and visualization of a phylo-
genetic tree with multiple sequence alignment (Huerta-
Cepas et al. 2016). The super-matrix-based construction 
mode of ETE permits building and concatenating the 
alignments with a phylogenetic tree of species. In addition, 
the visualization of the conserved amino acid residues of 
Dab1 adaptor protein among the eleven species was made 
by the MView tool of the European Molecular Biology 
Laboratory-European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-
EBI) (Madeira et al. 2022).
Prediction of O‑linked β‑GlcNAcylation 
and phosphorylation sites
The prediction of O-linked β-GlcNAcylation and phos-
phorylation sites of Dab1 adaptor protein were analyzed 
by YinOYang and NetPhos 3.1 servers, respectively, based 
on artificial neural networks (Gupta and Brunak 2001). 
Once the protein sequences of Dab1 in FASTA format 
uploaded to the servers had been analyzed, the obtained 
results were considered potential glycosylation and phos-
phorylation sites. After the prediction had been calculated, 
a threshold value above 0.5 was considered the potential 
phosphorylation site. Since the acceptor site of O-linked 
β-GlcNAcylation does not display a consensus sequence, 
a constant threshold was not used, and a prediction value 
above the site-specific thresholds was considered poten-
tial O-linked β-GlcNAcylation sites according to server 
prediction. The NetPhos 3.1 server also determined the 
possible kinases responsible for the phosphorylation on 
the Dab1 protein sequence (AAH67445.1).

Three‑dimensional protein modeling 
and visualization
The three-dimensional (3D) model of Dab1 adaptor protein 
(AAH67445.1) was constructed by the QUARK, which is a 
computational algorithm for ab initio protein structure pre-
diction and protein-peptide folding (Mortuza et al. 2021; 
Xu and Zhang 2012). The QUARK model of Dab1 was 
built from small fragments (~ 20 residue longs) by replica-
exchange Monte Carlo simulation under the guidance of an 
atomic-level knowledge-based force field. Since no global 
template information was used in the QUARK, the server is 
suitable for protein modeling that does not have homologous 
templates in the PDB library like Dab1. In addition, the two-
dimensional (2D) structure and surface accessibility ratio 
of the constructed model was visualized by POLYVIEW, 
which is a flexible visualization tool for 2D protein struc-
ture (Porollo et al. 2004). The model quality assessment of 
contracted 3D models of Dab1 was analyzed by ModFOLD 
(McGuffin et al. 2018) and Ramachandran Plot Assessment 
(RAMPAGE) servers (Anderson et al. 2005). The con-
structed 3D structures of the models were visualized, and 
the vacuum electrostatics were calculated by PyMOL, an 
open-source molecular visualization software (Schrodinger 
2015).
Results
Multiple sequence alignment of Dab1 proteins
The multiple sequence alignment of Dab1 proteins per-
formed between eleven species had shown that Dab1 con-
sists of a highly conserved protein sequence between these 
vertebrate species (Table 1). The percentage of identities of 
the human Dab1 sequence ranged from 92 to 80% among 
these sequences—the phylogenetic relationship of these 
species combined with multiple sequence alignments. The 
phylogenetically close species contained many conserved 
common domains and residues (Fig. 1). We found that the 
human Dab1 proteins also had unpaired regions compared 
to other Dab1 sequences shown in Fig. 1. The lengths of 
the unpaired regions of Dab1 were from ~ 35 to ~ 2 amino 
acids. These regions mainly existed in the central part of 
the protein.

Discussion
Dab1 is an adaptor protein predicted as approximately 555 
amino acids in length (Rice and Curran 2003). Multiple 
alternatively spliced Dab1 isoforms have been identified 
in various species, including human, mouse, chicken, pig, 
lizard, and zebrafish (Gao and Godbout 2013). The splicing 
events of Dab1 affect exons majorly encoding the tyrosine 
phosphorylation motifs (Gao et al. 2012; Katyal and God-
bout 2004). Human Dab1 sequence has a high similarity per-
centage of amino acids compared to other investigated spe-
cies at ratios above 80% (Table 1). Interestingly, the human 
Dab1 protein may contain an unpaired/absent sequence 
composed of ~ 35 amino acids, which exist from 184 to 220

phosphorylation sites in other species, chicken (Katyal et al. 
2007) zebrafish (Costagli et al. 2006), pig (Long et al. 2011), 
and mouse (Zhang et al. 2018) as our predictions (Suppl. 1). 
It may show that the Dab1 protein can be regulated in these 
phosphorylation sites for its activity evolutionary, which 
can affect the three-dimensional structure of Dab1 for their 
cellular function. In addition, the central and C-terminal 
regions of Dab1 have been found to have highly conserved 
amino acid residues (Fig. 4), which may have functional 
roles for Dab1 protein. However, it needs to be elucidated 
by experimental studies in detail.
In addition to Src family non-receptor tyrosine kinases, 
the Dab1 contains various phosphorylation sites (Fig. 2) 
with distinct types of kinases (Fig. 3). These kinases poten-
tially able to recognize Dab1 are significant regulators of 
cell signaling in a variety of cellular processes including 
apoptosis, migration, proliferation, DNA repair, and cell dif-
ferentiation (Ardito et al. 2017; Taylor and Kornev 2011). 
It is known that the Dab1 is cytoplasmic protein as an 
intracellular mediator of Reelin signaling process, but the 
treatment with leptomycin B (an inhibitor of the nuclear 
export receptor) results in nuclear accumulation of Dab1 
from cytoplasm to nucleus as a shuttling protein (Honda 
and Nakajima 2006). According to our predictions, Dab1 
contains two conserved sites ­(S258, ­T474) for CKI kinase 
(Fig. 4), which is responsible for phosphorylation of nucle-
ocytoplasmic shuttling proteins (Vielhaber et al. 2000). In 
addition, mouse Dab1 contains a nuclear localization signal 
(NLS) (localized between 20 and 36 amino acids) and two 
nuclear export signals (NES) (localized between 148–183 
and 460–479 amino acids, respectively). The nuclear export 
and import activity mainly depend on these NLC and NES 
signals that have been functionally observed in mouse Dab1 
(Honda and Nakajima 2006). Alternatively, a competition 
between O-β-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation impacts 
the nuclear localization of cytoplasmic proteins (Guinez 
et al. 2005; Seo et al. 2016). According to our results, Dab1 
contains conserved amino acid residues and crosstalk sites 
between O-β-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation close 
to mouse NLS, and NES signals topologically may show 
the potential modulatory function of the crosstalk sites for 
Dab1 shuttling. It is also shown that Dab1 possesses differ-
ent phosphorylation sites with various kinases that may have 
alternative biological processes in addition to cell motility 
and adhesion.
The O-β-GlcNAcylation has been shown to have cross-
talk with phosphorylation because they can modify same 
serine or threonine residues, and both are occupied at the 
same site or adjacent positions via steric hindrance (Wells 
et al. 2001). O-β-GlcNAcylation may regulate kinases or 
phosphatases at enzyme level and cause indirect changes 
on the phosphorylation (Wang et al. 2008). At the substrate 
level, the O-GlcNAcylation regulates the function of many 
cytoplasmic proteins, such as p53 and Tau. The stability of 
p53 is promoted with O-GlcNAcylation (W. H. Yang et al. 
2006), and the phosphorylation and the cytotoxicity of Tau 
are also decreased with O-GlcNAcylation in the mammalian 
brain (Yuzwa et al. 2012). In this view of reciprocal cross-
talk between phosphorylation and O-β-GlcNAcylation, Dab1 
contained many phosphorylation and O-β-GlcNAcylation 
sites (Fig. 2). Many of these sites were also highly con-
served and present in the same or adjacent sites (Figs. 4 and 
5). Enzymes responsible for phosphorylation at these con-
served O-β-GlcNAcylation sites regulate many signal trans-
duction processes, including nucleocytoplasmic shuttling, 
DNA repair, metabolism, apoptosis, and autophagy. It may 
show that the Dab1 functions in alternatively many sign-
aling pathways as well as neuronal migration. Reciprocal 
crosstalk between two main types of modifications may be 
important for protein characteristics. They may regulate the 
physicochemical parameters, including stability, solubility, 
protein–protein interactions, and resistance to proteolysis.
The PTB domains of the Dab1 have been well character-
ized with their crystallographic structure in mouse, which 
existed from 25 to 183 amino acids in length (Yun et al. 
2003). However, regions out of PTB of the Dab1 are not 
structurally modeled yet. The PTB region of Dab1 also 
contains characteristic foldings in their 3D protein structure 
(Farooq et al. 2003). According to our three-dimensional 
structure and two-dimensional assessment of the Dab1 
models, the Dab1 protein is composed of many disordered 
regions compared to their PTB domain (Fig. 5). It may per-
mit interaction capability of the same protein with many 
interaction partners functionally (Diella et al. 2008). In 
addition, this facilitates regulation of protein function via 
diverse post-translational modification owing to their confor-
mational flexibility (Bah et al. 2014). O-GlcNAcylation is an 
important regulatory modification that may have a reciprocal 
relationship with phosphorylation and may modulate many 
biological processes in eukaryotes (Hart 1997). These poten-
tial phosphorylation and O-β-GlcNAcylation sites predicted 
on the same residues are mainly located at the coil region 
of the Dab1 (Fig. 5A–D). Thereby, these modified sites 
may mediate potential protein–protein interactions events 
between Dab1 and its target protein as well as conforma-
tional flexibility of Dab1 protein.
Impaired regulation of glucose metabolism, especially 
the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP), is a com-
mon risk factor associated with many diseases, includ-
ing neurodegeneration, cardiovascular diseases, and 
cancer (Hart et al. 2011). UDP-GlcNAc produced as a 
product of the HBP is the high-energy substrate for O-β-
GlcNAcylation (Marshall et al. 2004). Dysregulation of

glucose metabolism via HBP directly impacts the O-β-
GlcNAc levels and is one of the significant causes of brain 
pathology (Banerjee et al. 2016). It is also known that 
many proteins involved in neurodegenerative diseases are 
O-β-GlcNAcylated/phosphorylated, and their crosstalk 
between them contributes to the pathophysiology of the 
diseases (Hart et al. 2011). When O-β-GlcNAcylation 
is chronically elevated, as in diabetes, the sugar modifi-
cation contributes directly to cardiomyopathy (Bolanle 
et al. 2021). In addition, many nuclear oncogenes (c-Myc) 
(Chou et al. 1995) or tumor suppressors (retinoblastoma 
gene) (Wells et al. 2011) are O-β-GlcNAcylated, and the 
crosstalk of the modification with phosphorylation modu-
lates various signaling pathways for tumor progression. 
Although a lot of detailed mechanism remains unknown, 
O-β-GlcNAcylation and their crosstalk with phospho-
rylation appear to be promising targets for therapeutics. 
Therefore, mapping both modification sites of Dab1 and 
possible kinases may help to understand molecular func-
tion of Dab1 with their potential therapeutic applications 
in these prevalent diseases.
In conclusion, we determined potential O-β-
GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation sites of human Dab1 
adaptor protein with their conserved residues among 
eleven species. Dab1 protein sequences were assessed 
from an evolutionary perspective. Potential kinases were 
determined with their recognition sites that may be glyco-
sylated with O-β-GlcNAcylation. In addition, the two- and 
three-dimensional structures of human Dab1 were con-
structed and calculated their vacuum electrostatic poten-
tial to analyze both modification types on protein struc-
ture. For further research, these determined sites should 
be experimentally confirmed, and their effects on protein 
structure and interactions should be investigated. There-
fore, an improved understanding of how these dynamic 
processes contribute to protein regulation is critical to 
improving treatment outcomes in various diseases. These 
findings may guide biochemical, genetic, and glyco-biol-
ogy based on further experiments about the Dab1 signal-
ing process.
