and Characterization of the O-GlcNAc Pattern for Functional 
Study 
Le´a El Hajjar, Clarisse Bridot, Marine Nguyen, Franc¸ois-Xavier Cantrelle, 
Isabelle Landrieu, and Caroline Smet-Nocca 
Abstract 
The neuronal microtubule-associated tau protein is characterized in vivo by a large number of post-
translational modiﬁcations along the entire primary sequence that modulates its function. The primary 
modiﬁcation of tau is phosphorylation of serine/threonine or tyrosine residues that is involved in the 
regulation of microtubule binding and polymerization. In neurodegenerative disorders referred to as 
tauopathies including Alzheimer’s disease, tau is abnormally hyperphosphorylated and forms ﬁbrillar 
inclusions in neurons progressing throughout different brain area during the course of the disease. The 
O-β-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) is another reversible post-translational modiﬁcation of ser-
ine/threonine residues that is installed and removed by the unique O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) and 
O-GlcNAc hydrolase (OGA), respectively. This modiﬁcation was described as a potential modulator of tau 
phosphorylation and functions in the physiopathology. Moreover, reducing protein O-GlcNAc levels in the 
brain upon treatment of tauopathy mouse models with an OGA inhibitor reveals a beneﬁcial effect on tau 
pathology and neurodegeneration. However, whether the role of tau O-GlcNAcylation is responsible of the 
protective effect against tau toxicity remains to be determined. The production of O-GlcNAc modiﬁed 
recombinant tau protein is a valuable tool for the investigations of the impact of O-GlcNAcylation on tau 
functions, modulation of interactions with partners and crosstalk with other post-translational modiﬁca-
tions, including but not restricted to phosphorylation. We describe here the in vitro O-GlcNAcylation of 
tau with recombinant OGT for which we provide an expression and puriﬁcation protocol. The use of the 
O-GlcNAc tau protein in functional studies requires the analytical characterization of the O-GlcNAc 
pattern. Here, we describe a method for the O-GlcNAc modiﬁcation of tau protein with recombinant 
OGT and the analytical characterization of the resulting O-GlcNAc pattern by a combination of methods 
for the overall characterization of tau O-GlcNAcylation by chemoenzymatic labeling and mass spectrome-
try, as well as the quantitative, site-speciﬁc pattern by NMR spectroscopy. 
Key words O-GlcNAcylation, O-GlcNAc transferase, NMR spectroscopy, Chemoenzymatic labeling,

Introduction 
The neuronal microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT), a 
phospho-protein involved in the polymerization and stabilization 
of the microtubule networks of axons, is the main component of 
the neuronal inclusions found in neurodegenerative diseases collec-
tively referred to as tauopathies, of which the most frequent form is 
sporadic Alzheimer’s disease (AD). The neuronal inclusions con-
tain aggregated, hyperphosphorylated forms of tau proteins that 
convert from a soluble, intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) into 
an ordered, ﬁbrillar state with amyloid structure and properties that 
overlap its functional microtubule-binding domain. Strikingly, the 
ﬁbril polymorphism, or strains, as revealed by recent cryo-electron 
microscopy structures at near-atomic resolution from patient brains 
and discrepancies with synthetic ﬁbrils made in vitro suggest a role 
of post-translational modiﬁcations and cofactors in the modulation 
of tau amyloid fold [1–7]. In addition to phosphorylation, Tau was 
found 
to 
be 
modiﬁed 
by 
O-β-linked 
N-acetylglucosamine 
(O-GlcNAc) modiﬁcation [8–13]. Since a deregulation of tau 
phosphorylation is a common feature in tauopathies, a link with a 
deregulation of the O-GlcNAc/phosphate balance was investigated 
as a potential mechanism to interfere with abnormal phosphoryla-
tion and aggregation of tau protein [11, 13–22]. Thiamet-G, a 
selective OGA inhibitor, was shown not only to modulate tau 
phosphorylation, but also improve tau pathology and neurodegen-
eration in AD mouse models. 
The O-GlcNAc modiﬁcation is a dynamic post-translational 
modiﬁcation corresponding to the addition and removal of a single 
sugar synthesized from glucose by the hexosamine biosynthesis 
pathway that affords UDP-GlcNAc as the end-product and donor 
in the O-GlcNAc transferase reaction. The cycling of O-GlcNAc 
involves the antagonist action of two unique enzymes in humans, 
the O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) and O-GlcNAc hydrolase 
(OGA), acting in the addition and removal of the GlcNAc moiety, 
respectively [23]. OGT is a soluble protein expressed under three 
isoforms acting in the nucleus and cytosol, or in mitochondria, in 
contrast to the vast majority of glycosyltransferases that are 
membrane-bound enzymes [24, 25]. The O-GlcNAc post-
translational modiﬁcation is therefore involved in the regulation 
of many nuclear, cytoplasmic, and mitochondrial proteins, hence 
intervening in a vast panel of biological functions including nuclear 
transport, transcription, signal transduction, cell cycle, and cellular 
response to stress [26]. Therefore, O-GlcNAc dysregulation is 
linked to diseases such as neurological disorders, diabetes, and 
cancers [27]. 
About half of the proteome is phosphorylated while the 
O-GlcNAcylation of proteins accounts for about 10–20% of the

proteome, and an overlap between the phospho-proteome and the 
human O-GlcNAcome was described [28]. Not only as an abun-
dant protein modiﬁcation, the O-GlcNAcylation has attracted 
much interest for its ability to modulate phosphorylation, protein– 
protein and protein–DNA interactions, and protein structure and 
function. 
Interestingly, 
a 
motif 
(pS/pT)P(V/A/T)(gS/gT) 
enriched in the phospho-proteome highlights a phosphorylation/ 
O-GlcNAcylation crosstalk in which phosphorylation (pS/pT) at 
P-3 position, a motif phosphorylated by proline-directed kinases, 
was shown to strongly inhibit O-GlcNAcylation of the adjacent 
(gS/gT) site [29]. However, the link between O-GlcNAcylation, 
and even more the site-speciﬁc O-GlcNAcylation, and changes in 
protein structure, function, modiﬁcation, and interactions, remains 
elusive in most instances such as in the case of tau protein. In 
particular, O-GlcNAcylation is often described as an antagonist 
modiﬁcation related to phosphorylation, and both protein post-
translational modiﬁcations may act reciprocally in the regulation of 
each other either by competing for the same site or by regulating 
the modiﬁcation of an adjacent or remote site [30–32]. However, 
the link between tau phosphorylation and O-GlcNAcylation 
remains a matter of debate [13, 17, 18].

The investigations of O-GlcNAc modiﬁcations in protein struc-
tures and functions suffer from several obstacles related to the 
detection and identiﬁcation of O-GlcNAc sites from cell and animal 
models, together with a lack of efﬁcient methods that reproduce 
the O-GlcNAc patterns in vitro. Indeed, the O-GlcNAc stoichiom-
etry is often low and requires methods for sample O-GlcNAc 
enrichment prior to analytical characterization (e.g., with MS-based 
methods). Moreover, tandem MS spectrometry for the identiﬁcation 
of O-GlcNAc sites must involve either a soft fragmentation method 
(such as electron transfer dissociation or electron capture dissocia-
tion) instead of conventional collision-activated dissociation or prior 
stabilization of the O-GlcNAc site by derivatization, such as the 
BEMAD (beta-elimination and Michael addition with dithiothreitol) 
approach [10, 33]. Enrichment of O-GlcNAc proteins in complex 
mixtures for subsequent analyses is a prerequisite as O-GlcNAc-
modiﬁed and unmodiﬁed forms of peptides usually co-elute during 
reverse phase HPLC while higher ionization efﬁciency of the 
unmodiﬁed peptides mitigates the signal of the O-GlcNAc-modiﬁed 
species. Therefore, analyses of O-GlcNAc peptides and proteins need 
methods that are speciﬁc of the single O-GlcNAc sugar and exhibit 
high binding afﬁnity, excluding the immunoafﬁnity puriﬁcation with 
pan-O-GlcNAc antibodies such as CTD110.6 or RL-2, or lectin 
afﬁnity chromatography (LAC) with the wheat germ agglutinin 
(WGA). Instead, chemical biology tools were efﬁciently developed 
to afford a vast panel of probes that can be speciﬁcally and stably 
linked to O-GlcNAc-modiﬁed substrates (for review, see [34–36]).

approach to label the O-GlcNAc-modiﬁed proteins with a galactose 
derivative that is amenable to a click chemistry reaction (Fig. 1).

donor to probe OGT variants, substrates, and inhibitors [41].

This chapter describes a method for the recombinant produc-
tion of the nucleocytoplasmic isoform of OGT (ncOGT), an 
O-GlcNAc transferase assay to evaluate the activity of recombinant 
ncOGT, and a protocol for the in vitro O-GlcNAcylation of tau 
protein and enrichment of the O-GlcNAc proteoforms. A combi-
nation of orthogonal methods for the analytical characterization of 
the O-GlcNAc proﬁle is provided exploiting mass spectrometry, 
chemoenzymatic reactions to detect the O-GlcNAc modiﬁcation 
of tau and resolve the distribution of O-GlcNAc proteoforms 
(Fig. 1), and the implementation of conventional 2D and 3D 
NMR experiments for the site-speciﬁc, quantitative description of 
the O-GlcNAc pattern.

3.1 
Production of 
Recombinant 15 N- and

Bacterial fermentations have to be performed following the best 
practices of sterile manipulations. 
3.1.1 
Day 1: Bacterial 
Transformation 
1. Transform Tau pET15b recombinant T7 expression plasmid 
into BL21(DE3) competent bacterial cells. 
2. Mix 50 μL of competent BL21(DE3) bacteria with 10 ng of the 
plasmid DNA in a 1.5 mL plastic tube. 
3. Incubate the mixture on ice for 30 min, followed by heat shock 
for 30 s at 42 °C. Place back the tube on ice for 5 min. 
4. Add 1 mL of LB medium at room temperature and incubate 
the bacterial suspension at 37 °C for 30 min. 
5. Spread 100 μL of the suspension at the surface of an LB plate 
containing 100 μg/mL of ampicillin antibiotics to select for the 
colonies having integrated the plasmid. 
6. Incubate the plate overnight at 37 °C. 
3.1.2 
Day 2: Bacterial 
Growth 
1. Start a small-scale bacterial culture of 20 mL by using few 
colonies from the selection plate as inoculum. 
2. Grow the Tau pET15b bacteria at 37 °C for 6 h in LB medium 
supplemented with 100 μg/mL of ampicillin, to reach satura-
tion corresponding to an approximate OD600 of 4.0. 
3. Meanwhile, reconstitute 2 L of M9 medium by addition of the 
supplements and isotopes following one of the labeling 
schemes indicated in Subheading 2.1 (see Note 5). 
4. Start a large-scale culture of 2 L by inoculating 20 mL of the 
LB saturated culture into 2 L of M9 medium. Perform the 
culture in 2 L-bafﬂed culture ﬂasks placed in an incubator 
programmed at 10 °C and 50 rpm for 14 h (see Notes 5 
and 11). 
5. Program the incubator to switch to 200 rpm and 37 °C after 
this period. 
3.1.3 
Day 3: Induction 
Phase 
1. Check the bacterial growth by measuring the OD at 600 nm of 
an aliquot of 1 mL of the culture. 
2. For the uniform 15 N or 15 N, 13 C-labeling, induce the protein 
production by addition of 500 μM IPTG when bacterial cul-
ture arrives at an OD600 of 0.8–1.0. 
3. Continue the bacterial culture for 3 h at 37 °C and 200 rpm. 
4. Collect bacterial pellet by centrifugation for 20 min at 5500× g 
in 1 L bottles.
